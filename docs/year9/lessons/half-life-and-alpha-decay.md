## Reading half-life from a graph

<p>The <strong>half-life</strong> is the time it takes for <strong>half</strong> of a radioactive sample to decay. On a graph of mass (or number of atoms) against time:</p>
<ol><li>Read the <strong>starting</strong> amount (where the curve meets the vertical axis).</li>
<li><strong>Halve</strong> it.</li>
<li>Go <strong>across</strong> from that value to the curve.</li>
<li>Go <strong>down</strong> to the time axis and read the time. That is the half-life.</li></ol>
<p>Check: halve again. The next half should take the <strong>same</strong> time again.</p>

## Describing the trend

<div class="full-answer"><p><strong>What a full answer includes: "Interpret the relationship (trend) between the variables"</strong></p>
<ol><li><strong>Name both variables and the direction:</strong> "As time increases, the mass of … decreases."</li>
<li><strong>Name the shape:</strong> an <strong>exponential decay</strong> (a negative, non-linear relationship): it falls quickly at first, then more and more slowly, and never quite reaches zero.</li>
<li><strong>Use data:</strong> quote values, e.g. it halves every … days (80 g → 40 g → 20 g).</li>
<li><strong>Give the reason:</strong> radioactive decay: in each half-life, half of the remaining unstable nuclei decay.</li></ol></div>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-describing-a-decay-trend"><summary>Worked example: describing the trend in a decay graph</summary>
<p>Interpret the relationship between the variables in the phosphorus-32 graph above.</p><hr>
<ul><li>As <strong>time increases</strong>, the <strong>mass of phosphorus-32 decreases</strong>.</li>
<li>It is an <strong>exponential decay</strong>: the mass falls quickly at first (40 g lost in the first 14 days) and then more slowly (only 10 g lost between 29 and 43 days).</li>
<li>The mass <strong>halves about every 14 days</strong>: 80 g → 40 g (14 days) → 20 g (29 days) → 10 g (43 days). So the half-life is about 14 days.</li>
<li>This happens because of <strong>radioactive decay</strong>: in each half-life, half of the phosphorus-32 nuclei that are left decay into another element (sulfur-32).</li></ul>
</details>

## Alpha decay

<p>An unstable nucleus can become more stable by giving off radiation. In <strong>alpha decay</strong> it throws out an <strong>alpha particle</strong>: <strong>2 protons and 2 neutrons</strong> (the same as a helium nucleus, <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>4</span><span>2</span></span>He</span>). Alpha decay happens in heavy nuclei with too many protons and neutrons.</p>
<ul><li>The <strong>atomic number goes down by 2</strong> (2 protons lost), so the atom becomes a <strong>new element</strong>.</li>
<li>The <strong>mass number goes down by 4</strong> (2 protons + 2 neutrons lost).</li></ul>
<p>The starting atom is the <strong>parent</strong>; the new atom is the <strong>daughter</strong>. Both sides of the equation must balance: the top numbers add up, and so do the bottom numbers.</p>

<table><thead><tr><th>Radiation</th><th>What it is</th><th>Atomic number</th><th>Mass number</th><th>Stopped by</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Alpha (α)</td><td>2 protons + 2 neutrons</td><td>−2</td><td>−4</td><td>paper or skin</td></tr>
<tr><td>Beta (β)</td><td>a fast electron from the nucleus</td><td>+1</td><td>no change</td><td>a few mm of aluminium</td></tr>
<tr><td>Gamma (γ)</td><td>high-energy electromagnetic radiation</td><td>no change</td><td>no change</td><td>thick lead or concrete (reduced, not fully stopped)</td></tr>
</tbody></table>

### Try it: step through an alpha decay

<div class="alpha-stepper"></div>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-alpha-decay-of-radium-226"><summary>Worked example: alpha decay of radium-226</summary>
<p>Explain what happens when radium-226 undergoes alpha decay. Show full working and a diagram.</p><hr>
<p><strong>Working</strong></p>
<ol><li>Radium's atomic number is 88 (periodic table), so radium-226 is <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>226</span><span>88</span></span>Ra</span>.</li>
<li>It gives off an alpha particle, <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>4</span><span>2</span></span>He</span>.</li>
<li>Mass number: 226 − 4 = <strong>222</strong>. Atomic number: 88 − 2 = <strong>86</strong>.</li>
<li>Atomic number 86 is <strong>radon (Rn)</strong>.</li></ol>
<p class="ad-eq"><span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>226</span><span>88</span></span>Ra</span> → <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>222</span><span>86</span></span>Rn</span> + <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>4</span><span>2</span></span>He</span></p>
<p>Check: top 226 = 222 + 4 ✓; bottom 88 = 86 + 2 ✓.</p>
<p><strong>Diagram</strong> (sketch it like this):</p>
<div class="flow">
<div class="step"><strong>Parent nucleus</strong>Radium-226: 88 protons, 138 neutrons</div>
<div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="step"><strong>Daughter nucleus</strong>Radon-222: 86 protons, 136 neutrons</div>
<div class="arrow">+</div>
<div class="step"><strong>Alpha particle</strong>2 protons + 2 neutrons, shot out of the nucleus</div>
</div>
<p><strong>Explanation:</strong> radium-226 is unstable. Its nucleus gives off an alpha particle (2 protons and 2 neutrons). It loses 2 protons, so its atomic number drops from 88 to 86 and it becomes a different element, radon. It loses 4 particles in total, so its mass number drops from 226 to 222. Radium-226 has become radon-222.</p>
</details>
