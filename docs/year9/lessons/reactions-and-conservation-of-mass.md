## Reactants and products

<p>In a chemical reaction, the bonds in the <strong>reactants</strong> (starting substances) break, the atoms <strong>rearrange</strong>, and new bonds form to make the <strong>products</strong> (new substances).</p>
<p style="text-align:center;"><strong>reactants → products</strong></p>
<p>Signs that a new substance has formed: a colour change, a gas (bubbles), a precipitate (a solid forming in a liquid), light, or a temperature change.</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Gas test</th><th>Result</th><th>Gas</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Bubble the gas through limewater</td><td>Limewater turns milky</td><td>carbon dioxide</td></tr>
<tr><td>Hold a lit splint at the mouth of the tube</td><td>Squeaky pop</td><td>hydrogen</td></tr>
<tr><td>Put a glowing splint into the gas</td><td>Splint relights</td><td>oxygen</td></tr>
</tbody></table>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-naming-reactants-and-products"><summary>Worked example: naming reactants and products</summary>
<p>A student heats white calcium carbonate powder (limestone) strongly. It turns into a white solid called calcium oxide, and a gas is given off that turns limewater milky. Identify the reactant and the products.</p><hr>
<ul><li><strong>Reactant</strong> (what you start with): calcium carbonate.</li>
<li><strong>Products</strong> (what is made): calcium oxide <em>and</em> carbon dioxide (the limewater test shows the gas is carbon dioxide).</li></ul>
<p>Word equation: <strong>calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide</strong>. Breaking one substance into simpler ones by heating is called <strong>thermal decomposition</strong>.</p>
</details>

## Balanced or not? Count the atoms

<p>Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction, so a correct equation has the <strong>same number of each kind of atom on both sides</strong>. That is a <strong>balanced</strong> equation.</p>
<ul><li>A small number written <strong>after</strong> a symbol counts only that atom: CO<sub>2</sub> has 1 C and 2 O.</li>
<li>A big number <strong>in front</strong> multiplies the whole formula: 2MgO is 2 Mg and 2 O.</li></ul>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-counting-atoms"><summary>Worked example: is it balanced?</summary>
<p>For each equation, count the atoms of each element on both sides and state whether it is balanced.</p><hr>
<p><strong>(a)</strong> CaCO<sub>3</sub> → CaO + CO<sub>2</sub></p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Element</th><th>Reactants</th><th>Products</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Ca</td><td>1</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>C</td><td>1</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>O</td><td>3</td><td>1 + 2 = 3</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Every element matches: <strong>balanced</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>(b)</strong> Mg + O<sub>2</sub> → MgO</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Element</th><th>Reactants</th><th>Products</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Mg</td><td>1</td><td>1</td></tr><tr><td>O</td><td>2</td><td>1</td></tr></tbody></table>
<p>Oxygen does not match: <strong>unbalanced</strong>. Fix it with numbers in front: <strong>2Mg + O<sub>2</sub> → 2MgO</strong> (Mg: 2 and 2; O: 2 and 2).</p>
<p><strong>(c)</strong> Splitting water by electrolysis (as in a Hofmann voltameter): 2H<sub>2</sub>O → 2H<sub>2</sub> + O<sub>2</sub>. H: 4 and 4; O: 2 and 2: <strong>balanced</strong>.</p>
</details>

## Conservation of mass

<p><strong>Law of conservation of mass:</strong> in a chemical reaction, the <strong>total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products</strong>. The atoms are only rearranged, so their total mass does not change.</p>
<div class="admonition tip"><p style="margin:0;">So a missing mass is found by <strong>subtraction</strong>: mass of reactants = mass of products.</p></div>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-calculating-a-missing-mass"><summary>Worked example: calculating a missing mass</summary>
<p><strong>(a)</strong> 25.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated until it has all decomposed. 14.0 g of calcium oxide is left. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide given off.</p><hr>
<p>calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide</p>
<p>25.0 g = 14.0 g + mass of carbon dioxide</p>
<p>mass of carbon dioxide = 25.0 − 14.0 = <strong>11.0 g</strong></p>
<hr>
<p><strong>(b)</strong> 2.4 g of magnesium ribbon burns in air and makes 4.0 g of magnesium oxide. What mass of oxygen joined the magnesium?</p>
<p>magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide</p>
<p>2.4 g + mass of oxygen = 4.0 g → mass of oxygen = 4.0 − 2.4 = <strong>1.6 g</strong></p>
<p>The ash is <em>heavier</em> than the ribbon, because oxygen from the air joined it. Mass was not created.</p>
</details>

## Open and closed systems

<ul><li>In an <strong>open system</strong>, matter can get in or out. A gas can escape into the air (the measured mass goes <strong>down</strong>), or a gas from the air can join in (the measured mass goes <strong>up</strong>).</li>
<li>In a <strong>closed system</strong> (for example a sealed flask), no matter gets in or out, so the measured mass <strong>stays the same</strong>.</li></ul>
<p>Mass is conserved in both. In an open system, some of it is just somewhere the balance can't measure.</p>

<div class="full-answer"><p><strong>What a full answer includes: "Open or closed system? Give a reason."</strong></p>
<ol><li><strong>Name it:</strong> open or closed.</li>
<li><strong>Reason:</strong> could matter (usually a gas) get in or out?</li>
<li><strong>Evidence:</strong> use the masses: did the measured mass change, and by how much?</li>
<li><strong>Link to conservation of mass:</strong> the "lost" (or gained) mass is a gas that left (or joined); the total mass of all the atoms did not change.</li></ol></div>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-open-or-closed-system"><summary>Worked example: open or closed?</summary>
<p>A student adds bicarbonate of soda to vinegar in a beaker on a balance. It fizzes. The total mass goes from 152.0 g to 150.9 g. Was this an open or closed system?</p><hr>
<p>It was an <strong>open system</strong>. The reaction made carbon dioxide gas (the fizzing), and the beaker was not sealed, so the gas escaped into the air. That is why the measured mass fell by 152.0 − 150.9 = <strong>1.1 g</strong>. The mass was not destroyed: 1.1 g of carbon dioxide left the beaker.</p>
<p>If the same reaction were done in a <strong>sealed</strong> bottle with a balloon over the top, the gas would be trapped. The mass would stay at 152.0 g: a <strong>closed system</strong>.</p>
</details>
