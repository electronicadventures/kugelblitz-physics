## Same element, different neutrons

<p><strong>Isotopes</strong> are atoms of the <strong>same element</strong> (same number of protons) with <strong>different numbers of neutrons</strong>. So they have different <strong>mass numbers</strong>.</p>
<p>An isotope is named by its element and mass number: <strong>magnesium-25</strong> has mass number 25. In nuclear notation it is <span class="nuc"><span class="nuc-n"><span>25</span><span>12</span></span>Mg</span>.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Same:</strong> protons, electrons (in a neutral atom), and so the same <strong>chemical properties</strong>. Isotopes of an element react in the same way.</li>
<li><strong>Different:</strong> neutrons, mass number, and sometimes <strong>stability</strong>. Some isotopes are <strong>radioactive</strong> (unstable): their nucleus breaks down over time.</li></ul>

## Describing the differences between two isotopes

<div class="full-answer"><p><strong>What a full answer includes: "Describe the differences between isotope A and isotope B"</strong></p>
<ol><li><strong>Neutrons:</strong> how many each has, and the difference (e.g. "1 more neutron").</li>
<li><strong>Mass number:</strong> which is heavier.</li>
<li><strong>Abundance:</strong> which is more common, quoting the data.</li>
<li><strong>Stability:</strong> is either one radioactive?</li>
<li><strong>What is the same:</strong> same protons and electrons, so same element and same chemical reactions.</li></ol>
<p>Always use numbers from the table. "It has more neutrons" is weaker than "it has 21 neutrons, one more than potassium-39's 20".</p></div>

<details class="example" id="worked-example-comparing-two-isotopes"><summary>Worked example: comparing potassium-39 and potassium-40</summary>
<p>Describe the differences between potassium-39 and potassium-40. Use the table to support your answer.</p><hr>
<ul><li>Potassium-40 has <strong>21 neutrons</strong>, <strong>one more</strong> than potassium-39 (20 neutrons), so its mass number is higher (40 compared with 39).</li>
<li>Potassium-39 is <strong>far more common</strong>: 93.3% of potassium atoms, compared with only about 0.01% for potassium-40.</li>
<li>Potassium-40 is <strong>radioactive</strong> (unstable), so it slowly decays. Potassium-39 is stable.</li>
<li>Both have <strong>19 protons</strong> and 19 electrons, so both are potassium and react in exactly the same way.</li></ul>
</details>

## Why are some isotopes rare?

<ul><li>A <strong>stable</strong> isotope lasts forever, so whatever amount formed is still here.</li>
<li>A <strong>radioactive</strong> isotope decays. Over billions of years much of it has turned into other elements, so less is left. The shorter its half-life, the faster it disappears.</li>
<li>Some isotopes were also made in much smaller amounts in the first place, in stars and supernovae.</li></ul>
<p>Potassium-40's half-life is about 1.25 billion years. Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, so most of the potassium-40 that Earth started with has already decayed.</p>
