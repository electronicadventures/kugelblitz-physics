## Quick recap: radioisotopes

<p>Some atoms are <strong>unstable</strong>. Their nucleus has the wrong mix of protons and neutrons, so sooner or later it breaks down and gives off radiation. This is <strong>radioactive decay</strong>.</p><p>An unstable isotope is called a <strong>radioisotope</strong>. When it decays, it turns into a different, <strong>stable</strong> atom.</p><p>You can never predict <em>when</em> one particular atom will decay. But with millions of atoms, the pattern is very predictable.</p>

## Half-life: a clock inside the atom

<p>The <strong>half-life</strong> is the time it takes for <strong>half</strong> of the radioactive atoms in a sample to decay.</p><ul><li>After 1 half-life, ½ is left.</li><li>After 2 half-lives, ¼ is left.</li><li>After 3 half-lives, ⅛ is left, and so on.</li></ul><p>Each radioisotope has its own half-life. Some are fractions of a second; some are billions of years.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> the half-life is <strong>constant</strong>. Heat, cold, pressure or how much you start with make no difference. That is why it works as a clock: if you know the half-life and how much is left, you can work out how much time has passed.</p></div>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: using half-life to tell the time</summary>
<p>A radioisotope has a half-life of 6 hours. A sample started with 640 radioactive atoms and now has 80. How much time has passed?</p><hr><p>Halve until you reach 80: 640 → 320 → 160 → 80. That is <strong>3 half-lives</strong>.</p><p>Time = 3 × 6 hours = <strong>18 hours</strong>.</p>
</details>

## How carbon dating works

<p><strong>Carbon-14</strong> is a radioisotope of carbon. Its half-life is about <strong>5730 years</strong>. It decays into stable nitrogen-14.</p>

<div class="flow">
<div class="step"><strong>1. Made in the air</strong>Cosmic rays hit nitrogen high in the atmosphere and make carbon-14. It joins with oxygen to make carbon dioxide.</div>
<div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="step"><strong>2. Taken in while alive</strong>Plants take in carbon dioxide. Animals eat the plants. Living things keep the same share of carbon-14 as the air.</div>
<div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="step"><strong>3. Death stops the top-up</strong>When the organism dies, it stops taking in carbon. No new carbon-14 arrives.</div>
<div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="step"><strong>4. The clock ticks</strong>The carbon-14 left inside decays, halving every 5730 years.</div>
<div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="step"><strong>5. Measure and count back</strong>Scientists compare the carbon-14 left with a living sample, count the half-lives and work out the age.</div>
</div>

<p>So carbon dating works only on things that were <strong>once alive</strong> (wood, bone, shell, cloth, charcoal). It also has a limit: after about <strong>50 000 years</strong> there is too little carbon-14 left to measure. Rocks, and fossils millions of years old, are dated with other radioisotopes that have much longer half-lives.</p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: carbon dating a bone</summary>
<p>A bone has ⅛ of the carbon-14 found in a living animal. How old is it?</p><hr><p>1 → ½ → ¼ → ⅛ is <strong>3 half-lives</strong>.</p><p>Age = 3 × 5730 = <strong>about 17 190 years</strong>.</p>
</details>

## Real science: wasp nests and Kimberley rock art

<p>Rock paintings in the Kimberley region of Western Australia are hard to date, because the paint usually has no carbon in it. Researchers working with the Traditional Owners found a clever way around this: mud wasps sometimes built nests on top of the paintings, or a painting was made over an old nest.</p><p>The nests contain tiny pieces of <strong>charcoal</strong>, which can be carbon dated. A nest <em>on top of</em> a painting must be younger than the painting, so it gives a minimum age. Using this method, scientists found that many paintings in the Gwion style are about <strong>12 000 years old</strong>.</p><p><a href="https://www.ansto.gov.au/news/dating-aboriginal-rock-art-using-mudwasp-nests" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ANSTO: dating Aboriginal rock art using mud wasp nests</a></p>

## Exit question

<p>In 2–3 sentences, explain to a friend how scientists could tell that a wooden spear is about 5700 years old. Use the words <em>carbon-14</em>, <em>half-life</em> and <em>decay</em>.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p>While the tree was alive it took in carbon-14 from the air. When it was cut down, it stopped taking in carbon, and the carbon-14 began to decay. The spear has about half the carbon-14 of living wood, so one half-life (about 5730 years) has passed.</p>
</details>
