## A two-way street

<p><strong>Technology enables science:</strong> a new tool lets scientists observe things nobody could see before. Galileo's telescope showed moons going around Jupiter. Radio dishes found the cosmic microwave background.</p><p><strong>Science enables technology:</strong> scientific understanding lets engineers build new tools. Newton understood that a lens splits white light into colours (blurring images), so he designed a telescope that uses a <strong>mirror</strong> instead. Understanding infrared light made the James Webb Space Telescope possible.</p><p>This feedback loop keeps going: new tool → new discoveries → new understanding → better tools.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> most big changes in our picture of the universe came soon after a new kind of instrument, or the same instrument made much bigger or moved into space.</p></div><p><img src="../../img/y10u07-windows.png" alt="Schematic of the electromagnetic spectrum from radio to X-ray, with bars showing that radio dishes see radio, Planck sees microwaves, JWST sees infrared, the eye and Galileo&#x27;s telescope see visible light, and Hubble sees from near-ultraviolet to near-infrared" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

## A timeline of technology

<p><img src="../../img/y10u07-timeline.png" alt="Timeline in two rows, not to scale. Row 2: 1961 and 1969 Parkes 64 m dish and the Apollo 11 TV relay; 1995 first planet found around a Sun-like star; 2013–2025 Gaia maps about 2 billion stars; 2015 LIGO detects gravitational waves; 2019 Event Horizon Telescope&#x27;s first black hole image; 2025 Vera C. Rubin Observatory first images" width="680" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><ul><li><strong>Before telescopes:</strong> Hipparchus (~130 BCE) catalogued about 850 stars by eye, with a brightness scale still used today. Ibn al-Haytham (~1020) showed by experiment that we see because light enters the eye, and studied lenses and mirrors: the science behind every telescope.</li><li><strong>1668, Newton's reflector; 1880s, photographic surveys:</strong> mirrors can be made far bigger than lenses, and photographic plates collect light for hours, recording stars far too faint for any eye.</li><li><strong>1912, Henrietta Leavitt:</strong> working from thousands of photographic plates, found that Cepheid stars can be used to measure distance.</li><li><strong>1932–1969, radio astronomy:</strong> Karl Jansky detected radio waves from the Milky Way. CSIRO's <strong>Parkes</strong> 64 m dish in NSW (1961) mapped radio sources and relayed TV of the Apollo 11 moonwalk (1969).</li><li><strong>1995, the first planet around a Sun-like star:</strong> found by measuring the star's tiny back-and-forth Doppler shift as the planet tugs on it. Today more than 5000 exoplanets are known.</li><li><strong>2013–2025, Gaia and 2025, Rubin:</strong> Gaia measured positions, distances and motions of about 2 billion stars from space. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory (Chile) will photograph the whole southern sky every few nights for 10 years, catching things that change.</li><li><strong>2015, LIGO and 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope:</strong> brand-new ways to observe. LIGO detected gravitational waves (ripples in space itself) from merging black holes. The EHT linked radio dishes around the world into one Earth-sized telescope to image the shadow of a black hole.</li></ul>

## How to critically analyse a development

<p>To <strong>critically analyse</strong> means to look at <em>both</em> the strengths and the weaknesses, then make a supported judgement. Five steps:</p><ol><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what is it, when?).</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what could it do that earlier tools could not?</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> what did we learn? Did it confirm, change or overturn earlier ideas?</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what could it NOT do? What problems or uncertainties did it have?</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important was it, compared with other developments? Give reasons.</li></ol><p>A strong answer is <strong>balanced</strong> (not just praise) and its judgement follows from the evidence. Words like "proved" are rarely accurate: one instrument usually provides strong <em>evidence</em> that, together with other evidence, changes scientists' minds.</p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: Newton's reflecting telescope (short version)</summary>
<p>Critically analyse the importance of Newton's reflecting telescope.</p><hr><p><strong>Identify:</strong> a small telescope Isaac Newton built in 1668 that used a curved <strong>mirror</strong> instead of a lens to collect light.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> lenses split white light into colours, giving blurry, rainbow-edged images. A mirror reflects all colours the same way, so the images are sharper. Mirrors can also be supported from behind, so they can be made much bigger than lenses.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Newton's own telescope (with a mirror about 3 cm across) made no big discoveries. But it showed the design worked, and it was based on his science of light and colour. Reflectors grew bigger and bigger: William Herschel discovered Uranus with one in 1781, and every large telescope today is a reflector.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> early mirrors were made of polished metal that reflected only about two-thirds of the light, tarnished quickly and were hard to shape accurately. This was not fixed until silver-coated glass mirrors in the 1850s.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> small immediate impact, but enormous long-term impact: it made today's giant telescopes possible. It is also a clear case of <em>science enabling technology</em>: Newton's understanding of colour led to a better design.</p>
</details>

## Guided example

<p>In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole's surroundings, in galaxy M87. Newspapers called it "the first photo of a black hole". Is that accurate? Use what the technology did and its limitations.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>What it did:</strong> eight radio observatories around the world were linked so they worked like one Earth-sized dish. That gave fine enough detail to see a glowing ring of hot gas around the black hole, with a dark centre where light cannot escape.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> it is not a photo in the normal sense. It is radio data from many telescopes, combined by computers into an image, and it needed years of processing and checking by independent teams. The black hole itself gives off no light: we see its <em>shadow</em> against the gas.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> "photo" is too simple, but the image was strong evidence: its size matched the prediction from Einstein's theory of gravity for a black hole of that mass. So it was a major step, as long as we describe it accurately.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Critical analysis

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what and when)</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what it could do that earlier tools could not</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> discoveries, and whether they confirmed, changed or overturned earlier ideas</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what it could not do, problems or uncertainties</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important, with reasons and a comparison</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example (a non-astronomy case).</strong> Critically analyse how the light microscope changed our understanding of living things.</p><p><strong>Identify:</strong> early microscopes of the 1600s, especially Robert Hooke's (1665) and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's single-lens microscopes (1670s), which magnified up to about 270 times.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> they showed objects far too small for the eye, below about 0.1 mm.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Hooke saw tiny compartments in cork and named them "cells". Leeuwenhoek saw living single-celled organisms, bacteria, sperm cells and blood cells. Over the next 200 years, better microscopes led to <strong>cell theory</strong> (1830s–1850s): all living things are made of cells, and cells come from other cells. This overturned the idea that life could appear spontaneously from non-living matter and led to the germ theory of disease.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> early lenses gave blurry images with coloured fringes. Light microscopes cannot show anything smaller than about 0.2 µm, so viruses and the detail inside cells stayed hidden until the electron microscope (1930s). Specimens often had to be thin, stained or dead.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> one of the most important technologies in biology. It created the field of microbiology and made cell theory possible. But its impact took a long time: it needed better lenses, staining methods and many scientists' work, and the electron microscope was needed for the next big step. Its importance comes from opening up a whole new scale of life, rather than any single image.</p>

<p><em>Guided (use the prompts)</em></p>
<p><strong>2.</strong></p><p>Critically analyse the importance of the <strong>Parkes radio telescope</strong> (CSIRO, NSW, 64 m dish, opened 1961).<ul><li><strong>Identify:</strong> what is it, and what kind of light does it detect?</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what can radio waves show that visible light cannot? (Think: dust, day and night, cold gas.)</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> one or two examples (e.g. pinpointing the position of the first quasar in 1962 so it could be studied, mapping hydrogen gas in the Milky Way, finding pulsars, relaying Apollo 11).</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> how sharp are its images compared with an optical telescope? Radio interference?</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> compare with an optical telescope from the timeline.</li></ul></p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Identify:</strong> Parkes is a 64 m dish run by CSIRO near Parkes, NSW, opened in 1961. It detects radio waves, which are long-wavelength electromagnetic waves.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> radio waves pass through the dust that blocks visible light, so Parkes can study the centre of the Milky Way and dusty regions. It can work in daytime and through cloud, and it detects cold hydrogen gas and objects that give off little visible light.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> in 1962 it was used to pinpoint the position of the radio source 3C 273, allowing it to be identified as the first known quasar: an extremely bright, distant galaxy core. It mapped hydrogen gas in the Milky Way and nearby galaxies, and has found a large share of the pulsars known (spinning neutron stars). In 1969 it relayed TV of the Apollo 11 moonwalk to the world. These discoveries changed our picture of the universe, showing violent, energetic objects that visible light alone had missed.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> radio waves are very long, so even a 64 m dish gives much blurrier images than a small optical telescope. Sharp radio images need many dishes linked together. It can be swamped by interference from phones, microwaves and satellites, and it sees only one part of the spectrum.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> very important, especially for Australian science, because it opened up a "new window" on the universe rather than improving an old one. Compared with Newton's reflector and the reflecting telescopes that followed it, which made a familiar kind of light sharper and brighter, Parkes revealed objects that optical telescopes had not noticed at all. Its main weakness, poor sharpness, has been overcome by linking it with other dishes, so it is still in use more than 60 years later.</p>
</details>

<p><strong>3.</strong></p><p>In 2015, the LIGO detectors in the USA recorded gravitational waves for the first time. Critically analyse how important this technology is for our understanding of the universe.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Identify:</strong> LIGO is two huge detectors in the USA, about 3000 km apart. Each has two 4 km long arms at right angles. Lasers measure the length of the arms extremely precisely. On 14 September 2015 both detected the same signal. Australian scientists (e.g. at ANU and in OzGrav) helped build and run it.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> it detects <strong>gravitational waves</strong>, tiny ripples in space itself, predicted by Einstein in 1916. A passing wave changes the arm length by less than a thousandth of the width of a proton. Every earlier telescope detected some kind of light; LIGO detects something completely different, so it can "see" black holes, which give off no light.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> the 2015 signal came from two black holes, each about 30 times the mass of the Sun, merging about 1.3 billion light-years away. This confirmed Einstein's prediction and proved that pairs of black holes exist and merge. In 2017, LIGO and the Virgo detector caught two neutron stars colliding, and telescopes saw the flash: this showed that collisions like this make heavy elements such as gold. The discovery won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> it only detects extremely violent events, such as mergers of black holes and neutron stars. With only a few detectors it cannot pinpoint where a signal came from very precisely, so the source can be anywhere in a large patch of sky. It is so sensitive that earthquakes, ocean waves and even traffic cause noise, and each signal lasts less than a second to a few minutes.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> very important, because like radio astronomy in the 1930s it opened a completely new "window" rather than improving an old one. So far it studies a narrow range of events, so its impact on our overall picture of the universe is smaller than tools such as Gaia that map billions of stars. But combined with light from telescopes (as in 2017), it gives information no other technology can, and its importance will grow as more detectors come online.</p>
</details>
