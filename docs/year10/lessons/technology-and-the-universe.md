## A two-way street

<p><strong>Technology enables science:</strong> a new tool lets scientists observe things nobody could see before. Galileo's telescope showed moons going around Jupiter. Radio dishes found the cosmic microwave background.</p><p><strong>Science enables technology:</strong> scientific understanding lets engineers build new tools. Newton understood that a lens splits white light into colours (blurring images), so he designed a telescope that uses a <strong>mirror</strong> instead. Understanding infrared light made the James Webb Space Telescope possible.</p><p>This feedback loop keeps going: new tool → new discoveries → new understanding → better tools.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> most big changes in our picture of the universe came soon after a new kind of instrument, or the same instrument made much bigger or moved into space.</p></div><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/missions/webb/outreach/migrated/2018/STScI-01H3W8QBES46GXWSMNY54SR46V.png/jcr:content/renditions/Quarter%20Res.png" alt="NASA infographic of the electromagnetic spectrum from gamma rays to radio waves, with bars showing the wavelengths observed by the Hubble Space Telescope (ultraviolet, visible and a little infrared), the James Webb Space Telescope (red light and near and mid infrared) and the Spitzer Space Telescope (longer infrared)" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, Joseph Olmsted (STScI) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/the-electromagnetic-spectrum-with-hubble-webb-and-spitzer-highlights/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p>This NASA image shows the whole electromagnetic spectrum and which parts three space telescopes observe. Different tools see different kinds of light. Our atmosphere blocks most of the spectrum: there are only two clear <strong>windows</strong>: visible light and radio waves. That is why the eye, Galileo's telescope and radio dishes such as Parkes work from the ground, while X-ray, ultraviolet and most infrared telescopes (such as JWST) have to go into space. Planck went to space to measure the microwave background without the atmosphere getting in the way.</p>

## How to critically analyse a development

<p>To <strong>critically analyse</strong> means to look at <em>both</em> the strengths and the weaknesses, then make a supported judgement. Five steps:</p><ol><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what is it, when?).</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what could it do that earlier tools could not?</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> what did we learn? Did it confirm, change or overturn earlier ideas?</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what could it NOT do? What problems or uncertainties did it have?</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important was it, compared with other developments? Give reasons.</li></ol><p>A strong answer is <strong>balanced</strong> (not just praise) and its judgement follows from the evidence. Words like "proved" are rarely accurate: one instrument usually provides strong <em>evidence</em> that, together with other evidence, changes scientists' minds.</p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: Newton's reflecting telescope (short version)</summary>
<p>Critically analyse the importance of Newton's reflecting telescope.</p><hr><p><strong>Identify:</strong> a small telescope Isaac Newton built in 1668 that used a curved <strong>mirror</strong> instead of a lens to collect light.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> lenses split white light into colours, giving blurry, rainbow-edged images. A mirror reflects all colours the same way, so the images are sharper. Mirrors can also be supported from behind, so they can be made much bigger than lenses.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Newton's own telescope (with a mirror about 3 cm across) made no big discoveries. But it showed the design worked, and it was based on his science of light and colour. Reflectors grew bigger and bigger: William Herschel discovered Uranus with one in 1781, and every large telescope today is a reflector.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> early mirrors were made of polished metal that reflected only about two-thirds of the light, tarnished quickly and were hard to shape accurately. This was not fixed until silver-coated glass mirrors in the 1850s.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> small immediate impact, but enormous long-term impact: it made today's giant telescopes possible. It is also a clear case of <em>science enabling technology</em>: Newton's understanding of colour led to a better design.</p>
</details>

## Guided example

<p>In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope released the first image of a black hole's surroundings, in galaxy M87. Newspapers called it "the first photo of a black hole". Is that accurate? Use what the technology did and its limitations.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>What it did:</strong> eight radio observatories around the world were linked so they worked like one Earth-sized dish. That gave fine enough detail to see a glowing ring of hot gas around the black hole, with a dark centre where light cannot escape.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> it is not a photo in the normal sense. It is radio data from many telescopes, combined by computers into an image, and it needed years of processing and checking by independent teams. The black hole itself gives off no light: we see its <em>shadow</em> against the gas.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> "photo" is too simple, but the image was strong evidence: its size matched the prediction from Einstein's theory of gravity for a black hole of that mass. So it was a major step, as long as we describe it accurately.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Critical analysis

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what and when)</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what it could do that earlier tools could not</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> discoveries, and whether they confirmed, changed or overturned earlier ideas</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what it could not do, problems or uncertainties</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important, with reasons and a comparison</li><li>Aim for about <strong>100–150 words</strong>: one clear point for each part is enough.</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example (a non-astronomy case).</strong> Critically analyse how the light microscope changed our understanding of living things.</p><p><strong>Identify:</strong> early microscopes of the 1600s, especially Robert Hooke's (1665) and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's single-lens microscopes (1670s), which magnified up to about 270 times.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> they showed objects far too small for the eye, below about 0.1 mm.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Hooke saw tiny compartments in cork and named them "cells". Leeuwenhoek saw living single-celled organisms, bacteria, sperm cells and blood cells. Over the next 200 years, better microscopes led to <strong>cell theory</strong> (1830s–1850s): all living things are made of cells, and cells come from other cells. This overturned the idea that life could appear spontaneously from non-living matter and led to the germ theory of disease.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> early lenses gave blurry images with coloured fringes. Light microscopes cannot show anything smaller than about 0.2 µm, so viruses and the detail inside cells stayed hidden until the electron microscope (1930s). Specimens often had to be thin, stained or dead.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> one of the most important technologies in biology. It created the field of microbiology and made cell theory possible. But its impact took a long time: it needed better lenses, staining methods and many scientists' work, and the electron microscope was needed for the next big step. Its importance comes from opening up a whole new scale of life, rather than any single image.</p>

<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p>Parkes is CSIRO's 64 m radio dish in NSW, opened in 1961. It detects radio waves, which pass through dust and cloud, so it can study objects that visible light cannot reach and work day and night. In 1962 it pinpointed the radio source 3C 273, which let astronomers identify the first quasar, a hugely bright, distant galaxy core that optical telescopes had not recognised. It has also discovered hundreds of pulsars. Newton's reflector made a familiar kind of light sharper, but Parkes opened a new window on the universe and revealed objects no one knew existed.</p>

<p><strong>3.</strong></p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0 0 4px 0;'><strong>Fact box</strong></p><ul style='margin:0;'><li><strong>LIGO</strong> is two detectors in the USA, about 3000 km apart. <details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p>LIGO is two laser detectors in the USA that first recorded gravitational waves in 2015. Every earlier telescope detected some kind of light, but LIGO detects ripples in space itself, so it can study black holes, which give out no light.
