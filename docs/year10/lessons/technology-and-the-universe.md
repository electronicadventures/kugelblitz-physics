## A two-way street

<p><strong>Technology enables science:</strong> a new tool lets scientists observe things nobody could see before. Galileo's telescope showed moons going around Jupiter. Radio dishes found the cosmic microwave background.</p><p><strong>Science enables technology:</strong> scientific understanding lets engineers build new tools. Newton understood that a lens splits white light into colours (blurring images), so he designed a telescope that uses a <strong>mirror</strong> instead. Understanding infrared light made the James Webb Space Telescope possible.</p><p>This feedback loop keeps going: new tool → new discoveries → new understanding → better tools.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> most big changes in our picture of the universe came soon after a new kind of instrument, or the same instrument made much bigger or moved into space.</p></div><p><img src="../../img/y10u07-windows.png" alt="Schematic of the electromagnetic spectrum from radio to X-ray, with bars showing that radio dishes see radio, Planck sees microwaves, JWST sees infrared, the eye and Galileo&#x27;s telescope see visible light, and Hubble sees from near-ultraviolet to near-infrared" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

## A timeline of technology

<p><img src="../../img/y10u07-timeline.png" alt="Timeline in two rows, not to scale. Row 1: about 130 BCE Hipparchus star catalogue; about 1020 Ibn al-Haytham on light and vision; 1609–10 Galileo&#x27;s telescope, Jupiter&#x27;s moons and phases of Venus; 1668 Newton&#x27;s reflecting telescope; 1840s–1880s photography and star spectra; 1912 Leavitt&#x27;s Cepheid distances; 1917 Hooker 100-inch telescope. Row 2: 1932 and 1961 radio astronomy, Jansky and the Parkes dish; 1965 cosmic microwave background; 1990 Hubble Space Telescope; 2013–2025 Gaia maps about 2 billion stars; 2015 LIGO detects gravitational waves; 2021–22 JWST; 2025 Vera C. Rubin Observatory" width="680" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><ul><li><strong>~130 BCE, Hipparchus:</strong> catalogued the positions and brightness of about 850 stars by eye. His brightness scale (magnitudes) is still used.</li><li><strong>~1020, Ibn al-Haytham:</strong> showed by experiment that we see because light enters the eye, and studied lenses and mirrors: the science behind every telescope.</li><li><strong>1609–10, Galileo:</strong> saw four moons orbiting Jupiter (so not everything orbits Earth) and a full set of phases of Venus, which fits Venus orbiting the Sun. Strong evidence <em>for</em> a Sun-centred model, but not complete proof on its own.</li><li><strong>1668, Newton:</strong> reflecting telescope. Mirrors can be made much bigger than lenses and do not split colours.</li><li><strong>1840s–1880s, photography:</strong> long exposures collect light for hours, showing objects too faint for any eye, and record spectra permanently.</li><li><strong>1912, Henrietta Leavitt:</strong> working from thousands of photographic plates, found that Cepheid stars can measure distance.</li><li><strong>1917, Hooker 100-inch telescope (USA):</strong> the largest of its time. Hubble used it to show other galaxies exist (1924) and that the universe is expanding (1929).</li><li><strong>1932–1969, radio astronomy:</strong> Karl Jansky detected radio waves from the Milky Way (1932). CSIRO's <strong>Parkes</strong> 64 m dish in NSW (1961) mapped radio sources and relayed TV of the Apollo 11 moonwalk (1969).</li><li><strong>1965, the CMB:</strong> found with a radio antenna.</li><li><strong>1990, Hubble Space Telescope:</strong> above the blurring atmosphere. The Hubble Deep Field (1995) showed thousands of galaxies in a tiny patch of "empty" sky.</li><li><strong>2013–2025, Gaia:</strong> measured positions, distances and motions of about 2 billion stars using parallax from space.</li><li><strong>2015, LIGO:</strong> detected gravitational waves from two merging black holes. Not light at all: a new way to observe the universe.</li><li><strong>2021–22, JWST:</strong> launched 25 December 2021; first images July 2022. Sees in infrared, through dust and to some of the earliest galaxies.</li><li><strong>2025, Vera C. Rubin Observatory (Chile):</strong> first images June 2025. Will photograph the whole southern sky every few nights for 10 years, catching things that change.</li></ul>

## How to critically analyse a development

<p>To <strong>critically analyse</strong> means to look at <em>both</em> the strengths and the weaknesses, then make a supported judgement. Five steps:</p><ol><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what is it, when?).</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what could it do that earlier tools could not?</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> what did we learn? Did it confirm, change or overturn earlier ideas?</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what could it NOT do? What problems or uncertainties did it have?</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important was it, compared with other developments? Give reasons.</li></ol><p>A strong answer is <strong>balanced</strong> (not just praise) and its judgement follows from the evidence. Words like "proved" are rarely accurate: one instrument usually provides strong <em>evidence</em> that, together with other evidence, changes scientists' minds.</p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: the Hooker telescope (short version)</summary>
<p>Critically analyse the importance of the Hooker 100-inch telescope.</p><hr><p><strong>Identify:</strong> a 100-inch (2.5 m) reflecting telescope, completed in 1917.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> its large mirror collected more light than any earlier telescope, so it could pick out individual stars, including Cepheids, in faint fuzzy "nebulae".</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Hubble used Leavitt's Cepheid method on these stars to show the Andromeda "nebula" is far outside the Milky Way: a separate galaxy (1924). With redshifts, he showed farther galaxies recede faster (1929). This overturned the idea that the Milky Way was the whole universe.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> it was on the ground, so the atmosphere blurred images. It only saw visible light. Hubble's early distances were too small (his expansion rate was about 7 times too big) because Cepheids were not yet fully understood.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> extremely important: it changed the size of the known universe and led to the idea of expansion. But it worked together with Leavitt's method and Slipher's redshifts. Without those, the telescope alone would not have been enough.</p>
</details>

## Guided example

<p>Galileo's 1610 observations are often described as having "proved" that Earth orbits the Sun. Is that accurate? Use what he observed and its limitations.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>What he observed:</strong> moons orbiting Jupiter, so not everything orbits Earth; and Venus showing a full set of phases, from crescent to nearly full, which fits Venus orbiting the Sun.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> the phases of Venus ruled out the old Earth-centred model of Ptolemy, but they also fit Tycho Brahe's model, in which the planets orbit the Sun while the Sun orbits a still Earth. His small telescope could not detect stellar parallax, which would show Earth moving.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> "proved" is too strong. Galileo provided strong <strong>evidence that supported</strong> a Sun-centred model and ruled out Ptolemy's model. Stronger evidence came later: Kepler's accurate orbits, Newton's gravity, and finally stellar parallax (1838) and the aberration of starlight (1729), which showed Earth moves.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Critical analysis

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Identify</strong> the technology (what and when)</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what it could do that earlier tools could not</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> discoveries, and whether they confirmed, changed or overturned earlier ideas</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> what it could not do, problems or uncertainties</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> how important, with reasons and a comparison</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example (a non-astronomy case).</strong> Critically analyse how the light microscope changed our understanding of living things.</p><p><strong>Identify:</strong> early microscopes of the 1600s, especially Robert Hooke's (1665) and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek's single-lens microscopes (1670s), which magnified up to about 270 times.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> they showed objects far too small for the eye, below about 0.1 mm.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Hooke saw tiny compartments in cork and named them "cells". Leeuwenhoek saw living single-celled organisms, bacteria, sperm cells and blood cells. Over the next 200 years, better microscopes led to <strong>cell theory</strong> (1830s–1850s): all living things are made of cells, and cells come from other cells. This overturned the idea that life could appear spontaneously from non-living matter and led to the germ theory of disease.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> early lenses gave blurry images with coloured fringes. Light microscopes cannot show anything smaller than about 0.2 µm, so viruses and the detail inside cells stayed hidden until the electron microscope (1930s). Specimens often had to be thin, stained or dead.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> one of the most important technologies in biology. It created the field of microbiology and made cell theory possible. But its impact took a long time: it needed better lenses, staining methods and many scientists' work, and the electron microscope was needed for the next big step. Its importance comes from opening up a whole new scale of life, rather than any single image.</p>

<p><strong>2.</strong></p><p>Critically analyse the importance of the <strong>Parkes radio telescope</strong> (CSIRO, NSW, 64 m dish, opened 1961).<ul><li><strong>Identify:</strong> what is it, and what kind of light does it detect?</li><li><strong>New ability:</strong> what can radio waves show that visible light cannot? (Think: dust, day and night, cold gas.)</li><li><strong>What it showed:</strong> one or two examples (e.g. pinpointing the position of the first quasar in 1962 so it could be studied, mapping hydrogen gas in the Milky Way, finding pulsars, relaying Apollo 11).</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> how sharp are its images compared with an optical telescope? Radio interference?</li><li><strong>Judgement:</strong> compare with an optical telescope from the timeline.</li></ul></p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Identify:</strong> Parkes is a 64 m dish run by CSIRO near Parkes, NSW, opened in 1961. It detects radio waves, which are long-wavelength electromagnetic waves.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> radio waves pass through the dust that blocks visible light, so Parkes can study the centre of the Milky Way and dusty regions. It can work in daytime and through cloud, and it detects cold hydrogen gas and objects that give off little visible light.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> in 1962 it was used to pinpoint the position of the radio source 3C 273, allowing it to be identified as the first known quasar: an extremely bright, distant galaxy core. It mapped hydrogen gas in the Milky Way and nearby galaxies, and has found more than half of all known pulsars (spinning neutron stars). In 1969 it relayed TV of the Apollo 11 moonwalk to the world. These discoveries changed our picture of the universe, showing violent, energetic objects that visible light alone had missed.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> radio waves are very long, so even a 64 m dish gives much blurrier images than a small optical telescope. Sharp radio images need many dishes linked together. It can be swamped by interference from phones, microwaves and satellites, and it sees only one part of the spectrum.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> very important, especially for Australian science, because it opened up a "new window" on the universe rather than improving an old one. Compared with the Hooker telescope, which showed galaxies more clearly, Parkes revealed objects that optical telescopes had not noticed at all. Its main weakness, poor sharpness, has been overcome by linking it with other dishes, so it is still in use more than 60 years later.</p>
</details>

<p><strong>3.</strong></p><p>Both the Hubble Space Telescope (1990) and the James Webb Space Telescope (2021) have changed our understanding of the universe. Critically analyse JWST's contribution compared with Hubble's.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Identify:</strong> Hubble (launched 1990) orbits about 540 km above Earth with a 2.4 m mirror and sees near-ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light. JWST (launched 25 December 2021) orbits the Sun about 1.5 million km from Earth with a 6.5 m segmented gold-coated mirror and sees mainly infrared light.</p><p><strong>New ability:</strong> JWST's larger mirror collects about 6 times as much light. Because it sees infrared, it can look through dust clouds into places where stars and planets are forming. Light from the most distant galaxies has been redshifted from visible into infrared, so JWST can see galaxies from only a few hundred million years after the big bang, earlier than Hubble could reach.</p><p><strong>What it showed:</strong> Hubble's Deep Field (1995) showed thousands of galaxies in a tiny patch of sky, and Hubble helped measure the expansion rate and discover that the expansion is speeding up (1998). JWST has found bright galaxies earlier than many models expected, which is causing scientists to revise ideas about how quickly the first galaxies formed. It also measures molecules such as water and carbon dioxide in the atmospheres of exoplanets.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> JWST is too far away to repair, while Hubble was serviced five times by astronauts. JWST must be kept extremely cold, cost about US$10 billion and was years late. It cannot see ultraviolet light, so Hubble remains better for hot young stars and some visible-light work. Early JWST distance estimates for some galaxies have needed checking with spectra.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> JWST extends Hubble rather than replacing it. Hubble's long life and repairs made it one of the most productive telescopes ever and showed the importance of getting above the atmosphere. JWST is already changing our understanding of the earliest galaxies in ways Hubble could not, because of its infrared vision. Overall, JWST is likely to have the bigger impact on our understanding of the early universe, but Hubble has had the greater total impact so far.</p>
</details>
