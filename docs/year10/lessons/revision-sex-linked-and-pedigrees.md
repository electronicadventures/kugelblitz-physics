## Hook

<p><strong>New York, 1910.</strong> Thomas Hunt Morgan had been breeding fruit flies, which normally have red eyes. One day he found a single male with <strong>white</strong> eyes.</p><p>He bred it with a red-eyed female. All the offspring had red eyes. He bred those offspring together. White eyes came back, but <strong>every single white-eyed fly was male</strong>.</p><p><strong>Think–pair–share:</strong> what could make a trait turn up only in males? What is different about males' chromosomes? By the end of today you will be able to explain Morgan's result with a Punnett square.</p>

## Sex chromosomes and X-linked traits

<p>Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes. One pair is the <strong>sex chromosomes</strong>: females are usually <strong>XX</strong> and males are usually <strong>XY</strong>. The other 22 pairs are <strong>autosomes</strong>.</p><p>The X chromosome carries many genes. The Y carries very few. A trait whose gene is on the X is <strong>X-linked</strong> (sex-linked). We write the allele on the X: X<sup>A</sup> (dominant) or X<sup>a</sup> (recessive). The Y has no allele for these genes.</p><table style='border-collapse:collapse;margin:8px 0;'><tr><th style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;background:#f3f4f6;text-align:left;'></th><th <strong>carrier</strong> (has the allele, does not show the trait)</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'></td><td style='border:1px #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>X<sup>A</sup>Y</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>unaffected</td></tr><tr><td are affected more often:</strong> they have only one X, so one recessive allele is enough. There is no second X to hide it.</li><li><strong>Fathers</strong> give their X to <strong>every daughter</strong> and their Y to <strong>every son</strong>. So an X-linked trait is <strong>never passed from father to son</strong>.</li><li>Sons get their X from their <strong>mother</strong>.</li></ul><p><strong>Two X-linked recessive examples:</strong> white eyes in fruit flies (Morgan's flies), and <strong>Duchenne muscular dystrophy</strong>, a condition in which muscles gradually weaken. It affects mostly boys and is caused by a mutation in a gene on the X chromosome.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> X-linked recessive traits usually pass from a man, through his carrier daughters, to some of his grandsons.</p></div>

Each son has a <strong>50%</strong> chance of white eyes.</p><p><strong>All offspring:</strong> 1 box in 4 is white-eyed = <strong>25%</strong>, and every white-eyed fly is male. That is exactly what Morgan saw.</p><p><em>Thinking to copy:</em> check whether the question asks for the % of <strong>sons</strong>, of <strong>daughters</strong> or of <strong>all</strong> offspring. They give different answers.</p>
</details>

## Reading a pedigree

<p>A <strong>pedigree</strong> is a family tree that shows who has a trait. Squares are males, circles are females, shaded means affected, half-shaded (if used) means carrier. A line between a male and a female joins parents, and the line down leads to their children. <strong>Generations</strong> are numbered with Roman numerals (I, II, III) and people with numbers, so II-4 is the 4th person in generation II.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10b02-pedigree-xlinked.png" alt="Pedigree A over three generations. Generation I: affected male 1 and unaffected female 2. Generation II: their children are carrier female 2, unaffected male 3 and carrier female 4; female 2 has partner male 1 and female 4 has partner male 5. Generation III: children of II-1 and II-2 are affected male 1, unaffected female 2 and unaffected male 3; children of II-4 and II-5 are affected male 4 and unaffected female 5" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><img src="../../img/y10b02-pedigree-autosomal.png" alt="Pedigree B over three generations. Generation I: unaffected male 1 and unaffected female 2. Generation II: their children are unaffected male 1, unaffected female 2 and affected female 3; female 3 has unaffected partner male 4. Generation III: their children are unaffected female 1, affected male 2 and unaffected male 3" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Clues for deciding the pattern:</strong></p><table style='border-collapse:collapse;margin:8px 0;'><tr><th style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;background:#f3f4f6;text-align:left;'>Clue in the pedigree</th><th style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;background:#f3f4f6;text-align:left;'>What it suggests</th></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>Two unaffected parents have an affected child</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'><strong>recessive</strong> (both parents carry it)</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>Every affected person has at least one affected parent; two affected parents have an unaffected child</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'><strong>dominant</strong></td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>Mostly males affected; passed from a man through his daughters to grandsons; never father → son</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'><strong>X-linked recessive</strong></td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>An affected daughter has an unaffected father, or an affected mother has an unaffected son</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'><strong>not</strong> X-linked recessive, so <strong>autosomal</strong></td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'>Males and females affected about equally; father → son passing happens</td><td style='border:1px solid #9ca3af;padding:6px 10px;text-align:left;vertical-align:top;'><strong>autosomal</strong></td></tr></table>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: genotypes in Pedigree A</summary>
<p>Pedigree A shows an X-linked recessive trait. Write the genotypes of I-1, II-2, II-3 and III-1.</p><hr><p><strong>Start with the affected people, then work outwards.</strong></p><ul><li><strong>I-1</strong> is an affected male: X<sup>a</sup>Y.</li><li><strong>II-2</strong> is his daughter. She must get his X<sup>a</sup>. She is unaffected, so her other X is X<sup>A</sup>: X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup> (carrier).</li><li><strong>II-3</strong> is his son. Sons get Dad's Y, not his X, so II-3 got his X from his mother: X<sup>A</sup>Y, unaffected. This is the "never father → son" clue.</li><li><strong>III-1</strong> is an affected male. His Y came from his father (II-1), so his X<sup>a</sup> came from his carrier mother II-2: X<sup>a</sup>Y.</li></ul>
</details>

## Evaluating a claim

<p>To <strong>evaluate</strong> a claim, you judge how accurate it is using evidence. Don't just agree or disagree: break the claim into parts and test each one.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0 0 4px 0;'><strong>What a full answer includes:</strong></p><ol style='margin:0;'><li><strong>State</strong> the claim (and split it into its parts).</li><li><strong>Test</strong> each part against scientific evidence: genotypes, a Punnett square or a pedigree.</li><li>Say which parts are <strong>accurate</strong> and which are <strong>inaccurate</strong>, with a reason for each.</li><li>Give an overall <strong>judgement</strong>.</li></ol></div>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: "Red hair skips a generation, so it must be on the Y chromosome."</summary>
<p>Evaluate this claim. (In a simple model, red hair is recessive and its main gene is on an autosome.)</p><hr><p><strong>1. Claim:</strong> (a) red hair skips a generation; (b) so the gene must be on the Y chromosome.</p><p><strong>2–3. Part (a): partly accurate.</strong> Red hair is recessive, so two parents without red hair can both be carriers and have a red-haired child. It can look like it skips a generation. But it does not always skip: two red-haired parents usually have red-haired children.</p><p><strong>Part (b): inaccurate.</strong> Only males have a Y. A Y-linked trait would appear only in males and pass from a father to <strong>every</strong> son, so it could not skip a generation of males. Red-haired females are common, so the gene cannot be on the Y. Skipping generations is evidence for a <strong>recessive</strong> allele, not for the Y chromosome.</p><p><strong>4. Judgement:</strong> the observation is partly accurate, but the conclusion is <strong>inaccurate</strong>. The pattern fits an autosomal recessive trait.</p>
</details>

## Accurate or inaccurate? (pairs, 5 minutes)

<p>For each statement, decide <strong>accurate</strong> or <strong>inaccurate</strong> and give a one-line reason. Use a Punnett square if it helps.</p><ol><li>A boy always inherits his X chromosome from his mother.</li><li>A carrier female shows the X-linked recessive trait.</li><li>X-linked recessive traits are more common in males.</li><li>All the sons of a man with an X-linked recessive trait will have the trait.</li><li>A woman can have an X-linked recessive trait.</li></ol><p><em>Answers:</em> 1 accurate (his father gave him a Y). 2 inaccurate (her X<sup>A</sup> hides the X<sup>a</sup>). 3 accurate (one X, nothing to hide the allele). 4 inaccurate (sons get his Y, not his X). 5 accurate, if she inherits X<sup>a</sup> from both parents.</p>

## Guided example

<p>Look at <strong>Pedigree B</strong>. Is the trait dominant or recessive? Is it X-linked recessive or autosomal?<br><strong>Prompts:</strong> First look at I-1, I-2 and their daughter II-3. What does an affected child of two unaffected parents tell you? Then look at II-3's father (I-1) and her son III-3. If the trait were X-linked recessive, what would II-3's genotype be, and what would that mean for her father and her sons?</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>Recessive:</strong> I-1 and I-2 are unaffected but their daughter II-3 is affected. Both parents must carry a hidden recessive allele (Aa × Aa).</p><p><strong>Not X-linked:</strong> if it were X-linked recessive, II-3 would be X<sup>a</sup>X<sup>a</sup>. She gets one X from her father, so I-1 would be X<sup>a</sup>Y and affected, but he is not. Also, every son of an X<sup>a</sup>X<sup>a</sup> mother gets an X<sup>a</sup> and would be affected, but III-3 is unaffected.</p><p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> <strong>autosomal recessive</strong>. II-3 is aa. Her partner II-4 is unaffected, but their son III-2 is affected (aa), so II-4 must be <strong>Aa</strong>.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Sex-linked crosses, pedigrees and evaluating claims

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Sex-linked cross:</strong> both parents' genotypes written with X and Y (e.g. X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup>, X<sup>A</sup>Y); a Punnett square; the % for the group asked (sons, daughters or all offspring)</li><li><strong>Pedigree:</strong> start from affected people; use the clues (two unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; affected female with an unaffected father or son → not X-linked); name the person (e.g. II-3) when you use them as evidence</li><li><strong>Evaluate a claim:</strong> state the claim and split it into parts; test each part with evidence; say which parts are accurate or inaccurate and why; give an overall judgement</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example: sex-linked cross.</strong> Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is X-linked recessive. A woman who is a carrier and a man who does not have DMD are planning a family. What are the chances for their sons and daughters?</p><p><strong>Parents:</strong> X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup> × X<sup>A</sup>Y.</p><table style='border-collapse:collapse;text-align:center;margin:8px 0;font-size:1.05em;'><tr><td style='padding:6px 14px;'></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>A</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>Y</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>A</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>A</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>A</sup>Y</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>a</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>a</sup>Y</td></tr></table><p style='margin:0 0 8px 0;font-size:0.9em;color:#374151;'>Father's gametes across, mother's down.</p><p><strong>Daughters:</strong> X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>A</sup> or X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup>: 0% affected, 50% chance of being a carrier. <strong>Sons:</strong> X<sup>A</sup>Y or X<sup>a</sup>Y: <strong>50% chance</strong> each son has DMD. <strong>All children:</strong> 1 in 4 = 25%.</p><p>Families in this situation can talk to a <strong>genetic counsellor</strong>, who explains these chances and the options available.</p>

<p>Female white-eyed = X<sup>w</sup>X<sup>w</sup>; male red-eyed = X<sup>W</sup>Y.</p><table style='border-collapse:collapse;text-align:center;margin:8px 0;font-size:1.05em;'><tr><td style='padding:6px 14px;'></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>W</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>Y</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>w</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>W</sup>X<sup>w</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>w</sup>Y</td></tr><tr><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#eef2ff;font-weight:bold;'>X<sup>w</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>W</sup>X<sup>w</sup></td><td style='border:1px solid #6b7280;padding:6px 14px;background:#ffffff;'>X<sup>w</sup>Y</td></tr></table><p><strong>All daughters</strong> are X<sup>W</sup>X<sup>w</sup>: red-eyed carriers (they get Dad's X<sup>W</sup>). <strong>All sons</strong> are X<sup>w</sup>Y: white-eyed (their only X comes from Mum).</p>

<p><em>Guided (use the prompts)</em></p>
<p><strong>3.</strong></p><p>In that cross (white-eyed female × red-eyed male), what % of the <strong>sons</strong> have white eyes? (Type a number only.)<br><strong>Prompt:</strong> where does a son's X come from?</p>
<div class="sc-num" data-answer="100" data-tol="0.005"></div>
<details class="success"><summary>Worked solution</summary>
<p>A son's X comes from his mother, and she only has X<sup>w</sup>. So <strong>100%</strong> of sons are X<sup>w</sup>Y (white).</p>
</details>


<p><em>Your turn</em></p>
<p><strong>4.</strong></p><p>In <strong>Pedigree A</strong>, II-2 is a carrier (X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup>) and her partner II-1 is unaffected (X<sup>A</sup>Y). What is the % chance that their daughter <strong>III-2</strong> is a carrier? (Type a number only.)</p>
<div class="sc-num" data-answer="50" data-tol="0.005"></div>
<details class="success"><summary>Worked solution</summary>
<p>Daughters get X<sup>A</sup> from II-1, and X<sup>A</sup> or X<sup>a</sup> from II-2. So a daughter is X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>A</sup> or X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup>: <strong>50%</strong> chance of being a carrier.</p>
</details>


<p><em>Guided (use the prompts)</em></p>
<p><strong>5.</strong></p><p>Evaluate the claim: <em>"White eyes in fruit flies only ever appear in males."</em><br><strong>Prompts:</strong> First state the claim. Then ask: what genotype would a white-eyed female need? Could a cross produce that genotype? (Try a carrier female × white-eyed male.) Then say what is accurate, what is inaccurate, and give a judgement.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Claim:</strong> only male fruit flies can have white eyes.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> a white-eyed female would be X<sup>w</sup>X<sup>w</sup>. A carrier female (X<sup>W</sup>X<sup>w</sup>) × white-eyed male (X<sup>w</sup>Y) gives daughters X<sup>W</sup>X<sup>w</sup> or X<sup>w</sup>X<sup>w</sup>, so 50% of the daughters have white eyes.</p><p><strong>Accurate part:</strong> white eyes are <em>more common</em> in males, because males need only one X<sup>w</sup>. In Morgan's second-generation flies, all the white-eyed flies were male.</p><p><strong>Inaccurate part:</strong> "only ever": females can have white eyes if they inherit X<sup>w</sup> from both parents.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> inaccurate. </details>


<p><em>Your turn</em></p>
<p><strong>6.</strong></p><p>Evaluate the claim: <em>"Because a girl's father has an X-linked recessive condition, she will definitely have it too."</em></p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Claim:</strong> a daughter of an affected father will definitely be affected.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> the father is X<sup>a</sup>Y. Every daughter gets her father's X, so she definitely inherits X<sup>a</sup>. Whether she is affected depends on the X from her mother: if the mother is X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>A</sup>, the daughter is X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup> (a carrier, 0% chance affected); if the mother is X<sup>A</sup>X<sup>a</sup>, there is a 50% chance; only if the mother is X<sup>a</sup>X<sup>a</sup> is it 100%.</p><p><strong>Accurate part:</strong> she will definitely inherit the recessive allele (she is at least a carrier).</p><p><strong>Inaccurate part:</strong> she will not "definitely" have the condition, because one X<sup>A</sup> from her mother hides the X<sup>a</sup>.</p><p><strong>Judgement:</strong> inaccurate. She is definitely a carrier, but she has the condition only if her mother also passes on X<sup>a</sup>.</p>
</details>
