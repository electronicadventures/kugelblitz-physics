## Weather, climate and El Niño

<p><strong>Weather</strong> is the conditions in one place over hours or days: today's rain, tomorrow's wind. <strong>Climate</strong> is the average pattern of weather over about 30 years or more.</p><p>Across the Pacific, steady <strong>trade winds</strong> normally blow from east to west along the equator. They push warm surface water towards Australia and Indonesia, where it piles up. Warm water heats the air above it, so moist air rises, forms clouds and brings rain to northern Australia. High up, the air flows back east and sinks over South America. This loop of air is called the <strong>Walker circulation</strong>. Near South America, cold water rises from the deep to replace the water pushed west (<strong>upwelling</strong>).</p><p>This back-and-forth pattern is called the <strong>El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO)</strong>. It has three phases:</p><ul><li><strong>El Niño:</strong> the trade winds weaken. Warm water spreads east across the Pacific, and the rising air and rain move east with it. Eastern Australia often gets drier, hotter weather.</li><li><strong>La Niña:</strong> the reverse. Trade winds strengthen, even more warm water piles up near Australia, and eastern Australia is often wetter.</li><li><strong>Neutral:</strong> in between.</li></ul><p><img src="../../img/y10c02-enso.png" alt="Two side-view diagrams of the equatorial Pacific. Normal: strong trade winds blow west, warm water piles up near Australia and Indonesia, moist air rises and rains over northern Australia, air sinks over South America, cool water upwells near South America. El Niño: weak trade winds, warm water spreads east across the Pacific, rising moist air moves to the central and eastern Pacific, dry air sinks over Australia" width="620" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

## The 2026 El Niño and Queensland

<p>This El Niño has been building since June 2026. In October, NOAA's measurement of the central Pacific (called <strong>Niño-3.4</strong>) was <strong>+2.1 °C</strong> above normal. A strong-to-very-strong El Niño is likely through to about March 2027.</p><p>Typical El Niño effects in Australia include:</p><ul><li>a drier winter and spring in the east and south, with hotter days;</li><li>a longer, harsher fire season;</li><li>a later start to the northern wet season (monsoon);</li><li><strong>fewer tropical cyclones</strong>, especially near Queensland.</li></ul><p>These are <em>odds</em>, not certainties. El Niño makes dry, hot conditions more likely, but it does not guarantee a drought in any particular town. Other drivers, such as the Indian Ocean, also matter.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>For Queensland this summer:</strong> plan for more heatwave days and a higher fire risk, and do not assume "fewer cyclones" means "no cyclones". One cyclone can still hit.</p></div>

## Natural wiggles on a rising trend

<p>El Niño and La Niña are <strong>natural variability</strong>. They move heat between the ocean and the air, making some years hotter or cooler than others. They do not add energy to the Earth system overall.</p><p>Climate change is the <strong>long-term trend</strong>. The enhanced greenhouse effect keeps adding energy year after year. So El Niño years sit on top of an ever-higher baseline. That is why El Niño years such as 2016 and 2023–24 set global temperature records, and why a La Niña year now is often warmer than an El Niño year from the 1980s.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10c02-trend-wiggles.png" alt="Illustrative line graph from 1980 to 2025: yearly temperature wiggles up and down around a rising dashed trend line. El Niño years are peaks above the line, a La Niña year is a dip below it, and later La Niña years are still warmer than early El Niño years" width="580" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Back to the vote:</strong> the record warmth is <strong>both</strong>: El Niño on top of human-caused warming.</p>

## Responses: Australia's road to net zero

<p><strong>Net zero</strong> means any greenhouse gases still released are balanced by the same amount removed from the atmosphere (for example, by growing forests). Australia's targets are written into law and policy:</p><ul><li><strong>43%</strong> below 2005 emission levels by <strong>2030</strong>;</li><li><strong>62–70%</strong> below 2005 levels by <strong>2035</strong> (set in September 2025);</li><li><strong>net zero by 2050</strong>.</li></ul><p>The Net Zero Plan has <strong>six sector plans</strong>: electricity and energy, transport, industry, resources, the built environment, and agriculture and land. Examples range from more wind and solar on the grid to rooftop solar, home batteries and electric vehicles.</p><p>Whether a society adopts a strategy depends on more than the science: cost, who pays, jobs, reliability and what people value all matter.</p><p><strong>Explaining a strategy:</strong> show how it works as a chain: <strong>cause → effect → link to the goal</strong>. Each step must lead to the next.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10c02-heatpump-flow.png" alt="Flow chart in six steps: a school or home removes gas heaters and gas hot water; electric heat pumps are installed; heat pumps move heat from the outside air, turning 1 unit of electricity into about 3 to 4 units of heat; no gas is burned on site so no CO2 comes from the building itself; as the grid adds wind and solar the electricity makes less and less CO2; building emissions fall towards the 2030 and 2050 targets" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: the heat-pump chain in words</summary>
<p>Explain how switching a school's gas heating and hot water to electric heat pumps helps Australia reach net zero.</p><hr><p><strong>Cause:</strong> the school removes its gas heaters and gas hot-water system and installs electric <strong>heat pumps</strong> (reverse-cycle air conditioners and a heat-pump hot-water system). A heat pump does not burn anything: it moves heat from the outside air into the building or the water, so 1 unit of electricity delivers about 3–4 units of heat.</p><p><strong>Effect:</strong> no natural gas (methane) is burned on site, so the building itself stops releasing CO<sub>2</sub>. The heat pumps use electricity instead, and because they are so efficient, the extra electricity needed is small. As more wind and solar join the grid, each unit of that electricity comes with less and less CO<sub>2</sub>.</p><p><strong>Link to the goal:</strong> heating buildings and water is a big part of the built environment's emissions, so this helps meet 43% below 2005 levels by 2030 and net zero by 2050.</p><p><strong>Limitation (bonus):</strong> while some grid electricity still comes from coal and gas, the saving is smaller, and replacing working gas systems costs money up front.</p>
</details>

## Guided example

<p>A friend says: <em>"2026 was really hot because of El Niño, so it's got nothing to do with climate change."</em> Use data and the idea of a trend to respond.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>Agree with part of it:</strong> El Niño does make years hotter. It moves heat from the Pacific Ocean into the atmosphere, so global temperatures go up for a year or so.</p><p><strong>But:</strong> El Niño is a natural wiggle that comes and goes every few years. It cannot explain a rise that keeps going decade after decade. The long-term trend comes from the enhanced greenhouse effect: CO<sub>2</sub> has gone from about 280 ppm to about 426 ppm, and the 11 years from 2015 to 2025 were the 11 warmest on record, including La Niña years.</p><p><strong>Conclusion:</strong> it is both. El Niño years are hot <em>and</em> they are built on a higher baseline each time, which is why they keep setting records.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Explain: cause → effect → link to the goal

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li>names the strategy and what physically changes (the cause)</li><li>states which fuel or gas is reduced or removed, and from which source (the effect)</li><li>connects the steps in order so each one leads to the next (no jumps)</li><li>links to the goal: lower emissions, and Australia's targets (43% by 2030, net zero by 2050)</li><li>for a top answer: one limitation or condition (e.g. depends on how the electricity is made, cost, time)</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Strategy:</strong> switching a school's gas heating and hot water to electric heat pumps.</p><p><strong>Cause:</strong> gas heaters and the gas hot-water system are replaced by heat pumps, which move heat from the outside air instead of burning fuel (1 unit of electricity → about 3–4 units of heat).</p><p><strong>Effect:</strong> no gas is burned on site, so the building stops releasing CO<sub>2</sub>; the electricity it uses makes less and less CO<sub>2</sub> as the grid adds wind and solar.</p><p><strong>Link to the goal:</strong> lower building emissions help Australia reach 43% below 2005 levels by 2030 and net zero by 2050.</p><p><strong>Limitation:</strong> the saving is smaller while the grid still uses a lot of coal and gas, and the change costs money up front.</p>

<p><em>Guided (use the prompts)</em></p>
<p><strong>2.</strong></p><p>Rotting food and garden waste in landfill produces <strong>methane</strong> (CH<sub>4</sub>), a greenhouse gas that traps much more heat per tonne than CO<sub>2</sub>. Some landfills now <strong>capture the methane</strong> through pipes and burn it in an engine to make electricity.</p><p>Explain how this reduces greenhouse gas emissions. Use the prompts:</p><ol><li><strong>Cause:</strong> what happens to the methane instead of escaping to the air?</li><li><strong>Effect 1:</strong> burning methane turns it into CO<sub>2</sub> and water. Why is that better than releasing the methane?</li><li><strong>Effect 2:</strong> the electricity made goes into the grid. What does that replace?</li><li><strong>Link to the goal:</strong> which targets does this help Australia meet?</li></ol>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p>Pipes collect the methane from rotting waste before it escapes into the atmosphere. The methane is burned in an engine, which turns it into CO₂ and water. Methane traps much more heat than the same mass of CO₂, so releasing CO₂ instead of methane causes much less warming. The engine also makes electricity, which replaces some electricity from coal- or gas-fired power stations, so they burn less fossil fuel and release less CO₂. Both effects lower Australia's total emissions, helping meet 43% below 2005 levels by 2030 and net zero by 2050. Limitation: not all methane is captured, and it is better still to keep food waste out of landfill (e.g. composting).</p>
</details>


<p><em>Your turn</em></p>
<p><strong>3.</strong></p><p>A state government is closing its <strong>coal-fired power stations</strong> over the next ten years and replacing them with <strong>wind farms, large solar farms and pumped hydro</strong> storage.</p><p>Explain how this strategy helps Australia reach its emissions targets.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p>Coal-fired power stations burn coal, which releases carbon stored in the geosphere into the atmosphere as CO₂. Wind and solar farms make electricity without burning any fuel, so as each coal station closes, the CO₂ it released each year stops. Wind and solar do not generate all the time, so pumped hydro stores energy: when power is spare, water is pumped uphill, and it flows back down through turbines when power is needed. This keeps the supply reliable without burning coal or gas. Because electricity is one of Australia's biggest sources of emissions, replacing coal cuts total emissions a lot, helping meet 43% below 2005 levels by 2030, 62–70% by 2035 and net zero by 2050. It also makes other strategies (electric cars, heat pumps) cleaner, because they run on that electricity. Limitations: new transmission lines and storage are needed, it costs money and takes time, and coal-mining communities need new jobs.</p>
</details>
