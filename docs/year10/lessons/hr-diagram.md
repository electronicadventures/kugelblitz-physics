# Reading the H–R diagram

<span class="checklist-codes">AC9S10U03</span> <span class="checklist-codes">AC9S10I05</span>

!!! abstract "By the end of this lesson you should be able to"
    - Read the temperature and luminosity of a star from an H–R diagram, including the reversed temperature axis and the log scale.
    - Link a star's colour to its surface temperature.
    - Identify main-sequence stars, giants, supergiants and white dwarfs on the diagram.
    - Use the diagram to describe how a star changes as it ages.

## Hook

<p>Look at the night sky and every star is just a dot. Some dots are bright, some are faint, some are a little red, some a little blue. Can a dot tell you how old a star is, how big it is, or how it will die?</p><p>Around 1910, two astronomers, <strong>Ejnar Hertzsprung</strong> in Denmark and <strong>Henry Norris Russell</strong> in the USA, each plotted stars on a graph of <strong>brightness</strong> against <strong>temperature</strong>. They expected a random scatter. Instead, the stars fell into clear groups. That graph, the <strong>H–R diagram</strong>, is now the most important graph in astronomy.</p><p><strong>Quick poll:</strong> a star that is very bright <em>and</em> cool. Should it be big or small? Vote, then we will find out.</p>

## Reading the axes

<p>An H–R diagram is a scatter graph. Each dot is one star.</p><ul><li><strong>Vertical axis: luminosity</strong>, the total energy a star gives out each second, compared with the Sun (Sun = 1). A star with luminosity 100 gives out 100 times as much energy as the Sun. The axis uses a <strong>log scale</strong>: each gridline is 100 times the one below.</li><li><strong>Horizontal axis: surface temperature</strong> in kelvin (K). Watch out: it runs <strong>backwards</strong>. <strong>Hot is on the left</strong>, cool is on the right.</li></ul><p>Temperature also sets a star's <strong>colour</strong>. The hottest stars (above about 10 000 K) look blue-white, the Sun (about 5800 K) looks yellow-white, and cool stars (about 3000 K) look orange-red. The colour strip along the top of the diagram shows this.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u04-hr-diagram.png" alt="H–R diagram: luminosity from 0.0001 to 1 000 000 times the Sun on a log scale against surface temperature from 40 000 K on the left to about 2500 K on the right. The main sequence runs from top left to bottom right, with Spica, Vega, Sirius A, the Sun and Proxima Centauri on it. Giants Aldebaran and Arcturus are upper right; supergiants Rigel and Betelgeuse are at the top; white dwarf Sirius B is lower left." width="620" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> always read <strong>both axes and their units</strong> first. Top = brighter, bottom = dimmer, left = hotter, right = cooler.</p></div>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: reading Vega</summary>
<p>Use the diagram to describe Vega's temperature and luminosity, compared with the Sun.</p><hr><p><strong>Temperature:</strong> Vega sits just to the right of the 10 000 K gridline, so about 9000–10 000 K. That is hotter than the Sun (about 5800 K), so Vega looks blue-white.</p><p><strong>Luminosity:</strong> it sits a little below the 100 gridline (the scale is logarithmic), so about 40 times the Sun.</p><p><strong>Group:</strong> it is on the main-sequence band, so it is fusing hydrogen in its core like the Sun.</p>
</details>

## The main sequence

<p>About 90% of stars lie on a band running from <strong>top left</strong> to <strong>bottom right</strong>. This is the <strong>main sequence</strong>: stars that are fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. A star spends most of its life here.</p><ul><li><strong>Top left:</strong> hot, bright, blue stars (like Spica). These have the <strong>most mass</strong>. They burn their fuel fast and live only millions of years.</li><li><strong>Middle:</strong> stars like the Sun, which will spend about 10 billion years here.</li><li><strong>Bottom right:</strong> cool, dim <strong>red dwarfs</strong> (like Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun). They have the <strong>least mass</strong> and use fuel so slowly they can last for trillions of years, much longer than the universe has existed.</li></ul><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> along the main sequence, <strong>mass</strong> decides everything. More mass → hotter, brighter, shorter life. Less mass → cooler, dimmer, longer life.</p></div>

## Giants, supergiants and white dwarfs

<p>Stars off the main sequence are mostly stars near the <strong>end</strong> of their lives.</p><ul><li><strong>Giants</strong> (upper right): cool but bright. A cool surface gives out little light per square metre, so to be this bright they must be <strong>huge</strong>. Aldebaran is about 45 times as wide as the Sun.</li><li><strong>Supergiants</strong> (along the top): the brightest stars of all, such as Rigel (blue-white) and Betelgeuse (red). They are massive stars late in life, heading for a supernova.</li><li><strong>White dwarfs</strong> (lower left): hot but very dim, so they must be <strong>tiny</strong>, about the size of Earth. Sirius B, the faint partner of the bright star Sirius A, is one. It is the leftover core of a dead star.</li></ul><p>So you can work out <strong>size</strong> from position: bright + cool = big; dim + hot = small.</p>

## Stars move on the diagram

<p>A star does not stay at one spot. As it ages, its temperature and luminosity change, so its dot moves. Here is the Sun's future, matching the life cycle from last lesson.</p><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://www.esa.int/var/esa/storage/images/esa_multimedia/images/2022/08/the_sun_s_future/24394513-1-eng-GB/The_Sun_s_future_article.gif" alt="Animated H–R diagram made from Gaia data, tracing the path of a star like the Sun: it slowly brightens on the main sequence, becomes a red giant, and ends as a cool, dim white dwarf" width="600" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: ESA/Gaia/DPAC (CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO) · <a href="https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2022/08/The_Sun_s_future" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p style='font-size:90%;'>This animation from the European Space Agency was made by plotting real stars measured by the Gaia space telescope, then picking out the ones with the same mass and make-up as the Sun at different ages. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old now; it will be at its hottest at about 8 billion years, and become a red giant at about 10–11 billion years.</p><ol><li>In about 5 to 6 billion years the Sun leaves the main sequence and moves <strong>up and right</strong>: it becomes a cooler, much brighter <strong>red giant</strong>.</li><li>It throws off its outer layers (a planetary nebula), exposing its very hot core: the dot jumps to the <strong>left</strong>.</li><li>The core becomes a <strong>white dwarf</strong> and drops to the <strong>bottom left</strong>.</li><li>With no fusion, the white dwarf slowly <strong>cools and fades</strong>, moving down and right for billions of years.</li></ol>

## Guided example

<p>A star is measured at about 4000 K with a luminosity of about 300 times the Sun. Where would it sit on the H–R diagram, what group is it in, and what can you say about its size?</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>Step 1, temperature (x-axis):</strong> 4000 K is on the right-hand (cool) side, so it looks orange.</p><p><strong>Step 2, luminosity (y-axis):</strong> 300 is between the 100 and 10 000 gridlines, a little above 100. It is much brighter than the Sun.</p><p><strong>Step 3, group:</strong> cool and bright puts it in the upper right, in the <strong>giant</strong> region (a main-sequence star at 4000 K would be dimmer than the Sun).</p><p><strong>Step 4, size:</strong> a cool surface gives out less light per square metre than the Sun, so to be 300 times brighter it must have a much larger surface. It is a <strong>giant</strong>, many times wider than the Sun.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Reading data from an H–R diagram

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li>Read BOTH axes, with units: temperature (K, hot on the left) and luminosity (Sun = 1, log scale)</li><li>Give values or ranges read from the diagram, not just "hot" or "bright"</li><li>Compare with the Sun (about 5800 K, luminosity 1): how many times brighter or dimmer?</li><li>Name the group: main sequence, giant, supergiant or white dwarf</li><li>Use the data to support any conclusion (e.g. size, mass or future)</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example.</strong> Use the diagram below. <em>Describe Betelgeuse using data from the H–R diagram.</em></p><p><img src="../../img/y10u04-hr-diagram.png" alt="H–R diagram with real stars, including Betelgeuse in the upper right" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Model answer:</strong> Betelgeuse has a surface temperature of about 3500 K (between the 3000 K and 4000 K gridlines), which is cooler than the Sun's 5800 K, so it looks red. Its luminosity is about 100 000 (10<sup>5</sup>) times the Sun's, near the top of the diagram. Because it is cool but extremely bright, it must be enormous, so it is a <strong>red supergiant</strong>. Supergiants are massive stars late in their lives, so Betelgeuse will end as a supernova.</p><p><strong>Why it works:</strong> both axes read with units ✔ compared with the Sun ✔ group named ✔ conclusion (size, future) supported by the data ✔</p>

## Exit questions

<ol><li>Why does the temperature axis of an H–R diagram catch people out?</li><li>A star is hot and very dim. What group is it in, and what does that tell you about its size?</li><li>Describe, in order, how the Sun's position on the H–R diagram will change after it leaves the main sequence.</li></ol>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answers</summary>
<ol><li>It runs backwards: hot stars are on the left and cool stars on the right. It is also usually a log scale.</li><li>A white dwarf. Being hot but very dim means it has a very small surface: about the size of Earth.</li><li>Up and right as it becomes a red giant (brighter, cooler); left as it loses its outer layers and exposes the hot core; down to the bottom left as a white dwarf; then slowly down and right as the white dwarf cools and fades.</li></ol>
</details>
