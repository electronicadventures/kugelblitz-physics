## What the theory actually says

<p>Run the expansion backwards and everything gets closer together, hotter and denser. The <strong>big bang theory</strong> says:</p><ul><li>About <strong>13.8 billion years ago</strong>, the whole observable universe was in an extremely <strong>hot, dense</strong> state.</li><li>Since then, <strong>space itself has been expanding</strong> and the universe has been cooling.</li><li>As it cooled, particles, then atoms, then stars and galaxies formed.</li></ul><p>Three things it does <strong>not</strong> say:</p><ul><li><strong>Not an explosion into empty space.</strong> There was no outside to explode into. The expansion happened <em>everywhere at once</em>; every point moved apart from every other point.</li><li><strong>Not a centre.</strong> The big bang did not happen at one place in space. It happened to all of space.</li><li><strong>Not the very first instant.</strong> The theory is well tested from a tiny fraction of a second after the start. What happened at time zero, or "before", is still an open question.</li></ul><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> the big bang is the <em>expansion and cooling</em> of a hot, dense early universe, happening everywhere. The name comes from Fred Hoyle, a critic of the theory, on BBC radio in 1949. He is often said to have used "big bang" as a put-down (he later denied mocking it). Either way, it stuck.</p></div>

## Three lines of evidence

<p><strong>Evidence 1: the universe is expanding.</strong> Redshift shows galaxies moving apart, faster the farther away (last lesson). If they are moving apart now, they were closer together in the past.</p><p><strong>Evidence 2: the cosmic microwave background (CMB).</strong> For about <strong>380 000 years</strong>, the universe was so hot that light could not travel far: it kept bouncing off free electrons, like light in fog. When it cooled to about 3000 K, atoms formed, the fog cleared, and that light has been travelling ever since. Expansion has stretched it from visible and infrared light into <strong>microwaves</strong>, now matching a temperature of just <strong>2.7 K</strong> (−270 °C). It comes from <em>every direction</em>.</p><p>In <strong>1965</strong>, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were testing a large horn-shaped radio antenna in the USA and found a faint hiss they could not get rid of. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings (and evicted the pigeons). The hiss stayed, from every direction, day and night. It was the CMB, predicted by big bang scientists in the 1940s. They shared the 1978 Nobel Prize. Satellites have since mapped it in detail: <strong>COBE</strong> (1989), <strong>WMAP</strong> (2001) and <strong>Planck</strong> (2009).</p><p><strong>Evidence 3: the amounts of light elements.</strong> In the first few <strong>minutes</strong>, the universe was hot enough for nuclear fusion, but only briefly. The theory predicts this made ordinary matter about <strong>75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass</strong>, with tiny traces of others. The oldest gas and stars match this.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u06-elements.png" alt="Bar chart comparing the predicted and observed make-up of ordinary matter by mass: both about 75 percent hydrogen and 25 percent helium" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

## The rival theory that lost: steady state

<p>In 1948, Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle proposed the <strong>steady state theory</strong>. The universe expands, but it has <strong>no beginning</strong>: new matter is created very slowly to fill the gaps, so the universe always looks the same.</p><p>It was a good scientific theory, because it made <strong>testable predictions</strong>. They turned out wrong:</p><ul><li><strong>No CMB.</strong> Steady state predicts no leftover glow from a hot past. Penzias and Wilson found one.</li><li><strong>The distant universe looks different.</strong> Looking far away means looking back in time. Distant (early) regions contain far more <strong>quasars</strong> (very bright, young galaxy cores) and young, small galaxies than nearby regions. A steady state universe should look the same at all times.</li><li><strong>Helium.</strong> Stars alone cannot make as much helium as we observe. The big bang explains it.</li></ul><p>This is science working well: two theories, testable predictions, and the evidence decides.</p>

## A timeline, and how it might end

<p><img src="../../img/y10u06-timeline.png" alt="Timeline of the universe, not to scale: big bang; first nuclei in the first few minutes; atoms form and the CMB is released at about 380 000 years; first stars at about 100 to 200 million years; galaxies grow; Sun and Earth form 9.2 billion years after the big bang, 4.6 billion years ago; today at 13.8 billion years" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Will the expansion ever stop?</strong> Gravity pulls everything together, so astronomers expected the expansion to be <strong>slowing down</strong>. In <strong>1998</strong>, two teams measuring distant exploding stars (supernovae) found the opposite: the expansion is <strong>speeding up</strong>. One team was led by Brian Schmidt at the Australian National University (Nobel Prize 2011). Whatever causes this is called <strong>dark energy</strong>. We do not yet know what it is.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u06-fates.png" alt="Sketch of size of the universe against time: the past curve rises steeply at first, slows, then starts speeding up about 5 billion years ago; then three possible futures: Big Freeze keeps expanding faster, Big Rip expands so fast it tears apart, Big Crunch slows, stops and collapses" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><ul><li><strong>Big Freeze</strong> (heat death): expansion continues forever. Galaxies drift out of sight, stars burn out, everything cools towards absolute zero. <em>This fits the current evidence best.</em></li><li><strong>Big Rip:</strong> dark energy gets <em>stronger</em> and eventually tears apart galaxies, stars and even atoms.</li><li><strong>Big Crunch:</strong> expansion stops and reverses, and everything collapses. This would need gravity to win, or dark energy to fade and change sign.</li></ul><p><strong>What would decide it?</strong> Measuring whether dark energy stays constant over time, using more supernovae, galaxy maps and the CMB. Recent large surveys have found <em>hints</em> that dark energy may be changing, so this is an active question.</p>

## Evidence card sort

<p>On cards (or a table), sort these statements into <strong>supports big bang</strong>, <strong>supports steady state</strong>, or <strong>supports both</strong>:</p><ol><li>Distant galaxies are redshifted.</li><li>A faint microwave glow comes from every direction in the sky.</li><li>Ordinary matter is about 25% helium by mass.</li><li>There were more quasars billions of years ago than there are now.</li><li>The universe is expanding.</li></ol><p>Then: <strong>which single card was most decisive</strong> in deciding between the theories? Justify your choice in two sentences.</p><p><em>Answers:</em> 1 and 5 support both (both theories include expansion); 2, 3 and 4 support only the big bang. The CMB (card 2) is usually seen as decisive, because steady state has no way to explain it.</p>

## Guided example

<p>A friend says: "The big bang happened at one spot, and now everything is flying away from that spot. If we could find it, we'd find the centre of the universe." Use the evidence to explain what is wrong with this idea.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>Step 1: what do the observations show?</strong> Galaxies in <em>every</em> direction are moving away from us, faster the farther away. The CMB arrives from <em>every</em> direction, almost the same temperature everywhere (differences of about 1 part in 100 000).</p><p><strong>Step 2: what would a "one spot" explosion predict?</strong> We would see galaxies moving away from a particular point, and the CMB would be brighter from one direction (towards the spot) than others.</p><p><strong>Step 3: compare.</strong> The observations do not match that. They match space expanding <em>everywhere at once</em>, which looks the same from every galaxy (raisin bread). So there is no centre to find: every place in the universe was part of the hot, dense early state.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Justified prediction

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Prediction:</strong> a clear statement of what will happen</li><li><strong>Evidence:</strong> data or observations, with units where there are numbers</li><li><strong>Reasoning:</strong> the science that links the evidence to the prediction</li><li><strong>What could change it:</strong> an assumption you made, or new evidence that would change your prediction</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example (an everyday context).</strong> A cup of tea is left on a bench in a 22 °C room. Its temperature is measured every 5 minutes.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u06-tea.png" alt="Graph of tea temperature against time: 88 °C at 0 min, 64 °C at 10 min, 49 °C at 20 min, 39 °C at 30 min, 33 °C at 40 min, curving down towards the room temperature of 22 °C" width="500" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Question:</strong> Make a justified prediction of the tea's temperature after 60 minutes.</p><p><strong>Prediction:</strong> After 60 minutes the tea will be about 26 °C: close to, but slightly above, room temperature.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> The tea cooled from 88 °C to 64 °C in the first 10 minutes (a drop of 24 °C), but only from 43 °C to 33 °C between 25 and 40 minutes (a drop of 10 °C in 15 minutes). The curve is levelling off as it approaches the room temperature of 22 °C.</p><p><strong>Reasoning:</strong> Heat flows from the hot tea to the cooler room. The smaller the temperature difference, the slower the heat flows, so the cooling slows down. The tea can cool towards 22 °C but not below it. Following the flattening curve another 20 minutes gives a value in the mid-20s.</p><p><strong>What could change it:</strong> I assumed the room stays at 22 °C and nothing else changes. If someone added milk, put a lid on, or the air-conditioning came on, the prediction would change.</p>

<p><em>Guided (use the prompts)</em></p>
<p><strong>2.</strong></p><p>The star Alpha Centauri A has about <strong>1.1 times the mass of the Sun</strong> and is a main-sequence star fusing hydrogen in its core. Make a justified prediction about how its life will end. (Use the star life cycle from the scale and life cycles lesson.)<ul><li><strong>Prediction:</strong> what stages will it go through, and what will be left at the end?</li><li><strong>Evidence:</strong> what do we know about this star (mass, current stage)?</li><li><strong>Reasoning:</strong> why does mass decide a star's fate? What happens when core hydrogen runs out?</li><li><strong>What could change it:</strong> what if the mass measurement were very wrong, or it had a close companion star?</li></ul></p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Prediction:</strong> Alpha Centauri A will swell into a red giant, puff off its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and end as a white dwarf. It will not become a supernova, neutron star or black hole.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> It has about 1.1 times the Sun's mass and is on the main sequence, fusing hydrogen in its core, like the Sun.</p><p><strong>Reasoning:</strong> A star's mass decides how hot and squeezed its core can get. When core hydrogen runs out, the core contracts and heats, and the outer layers expand and cool: a red giant. Stars below about 8 solar masses cannot get hot enough to fuse elements all the way to iron, so they do not explode as supernovae. They lose their outer layers gently and leave a hot, dense core: a white dwarf. At 1.1 solar masses, Alpha Centauri A is well below this limit.</p><p><strong>What could change it:</strong> If its mass were actually above about 8 solar masses (very unlikely, given how it compares with the Sun), it would end as a supernova. It is in a system with another star (Alpha Centauri B); if they were very close, they could swap material and change the ending, but they are far enough apart that this is not expected.</p>
</details>


<p><em>Your turn</em></p>
<p><strong>3.</strong></p><p>Today the cosmic microwave background (CMB) has a temperature of 2.7 K. When it was released, about 380 000 years after the start, it was about 3000 K. Since then the universe has expanded about 1100 times. Predict what will happen to the temperature of the CMB over the next tens of billions of years, and justify your prediction.</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Model answer</summary>
<p><strong>Prediction:</strong> The CMB will keep getting colder, getting closer and closer to absolute zero (0 K) but never quite reaching it. Its wavelengths will keep getting longer, from microwaves towards radio waves.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> The CMB has cooled from about 3000 K to 2.7 K while the universe expanded about 1100 times (3000 ÷ 1100 ≈ 2.7). Redshift measurements show that the universe is still expanding, and since 1998 the evidence shows that the expansion is speeding up.</p><p><strong>Reasoning:</strong> As space expands, the light travelling through it is stretched to longer wavelengths. Longer-wavelength radiation matches a lower temperature, so the temperature falls in step with the stretching: if distances double, the CMB temperature halves. Because the expansion is continuing and speeding up, the stretching will continue, so the CMB will get colder and colder (for example, about 1.35 K once distances have doubled again).</p><p><strong>What could change it:</strong> This assumes the universe keeps expanding. If dark energy faded and the expansion reversed (a Big Crunch), the CMB would be squeezed to shorter wavelengths and would heat up again. New measurements of how dark energy changes over time would test this.</p>
</details>
