## Hook

<p><strong>Close your eyes.</strong> An ambulance is coming down the street with its siren on. It passes you and drives away.</p><ul><li>What happens to the <strong>pitch</strong> of the siren as it passes?</li><li>The siren itself never changed. So what did?</li></ul><p>Try it: put a small buzzer (switched on) inside a foam ball or a rolled-up sock and gently throw it back and forth between two people, or roll it past the class. Listen to the pitch as it comes towards you and goes away.</p><p><strong>Big question for today:</strong> could the same thing happen to <em>light</em>? And what would it tell us about galaxies millions of light-years away?</p>

## The Doppler effect: sound first

<p>A sound source sends out waves. Each crest spreads out like a ripple. If the source is <strong>still</strong>, the crests are evenly spaced in every direction.</p><p>If the source is <strong>moving</strong>, it chases the waves in front of it and runs away from the waves behind it.</p><ul><li><strong>In front:</strong> crests are squashed together. Shorter <strong>wavelength</strong> (distance between crests) → higher pitch.</li><li><strong>Behind:</strong> crests are stretched apart. Longer wavelength → lower pitch.</li></ul><p>This change in wavelength caused by motion is the <strong>Doppler effect</strong>. The siren never changes. What you hear depends on whether the source is coming towards you or moving away.</p><p><strong>Light does the same thing.</strong> In this NASA illustration, the middle spectrum is a star that is not moving towards or away from us. When the star moves <strong>towards</strong> us (top), its dark lines shift towards the blue end (<strong>blueshift</strong>). When it moves <strong>away</strong> (bottom), they shift towards the red end (<strong>redshift</strong>).</p><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/missions/webb/outreach/migrated/2021/STScI-01F8QPCFSQCCNRDW2VBTJF3QHM.png/jcr:content/renditions/cq5dam.web.1280.1280.png" alt="Three absorption spectra of a star: top, star moving towards us, dark lines shifted to bluer wavelengths; middle, star not moving towards or away, lines in their normal places; bottom, star moving away, lines shifted to redder wavelengths" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Illustration: NASA, ESA, CSA, Leah Hustak (STScI) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/doppler-shift-of-a-stars-spectrum/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure>

## Light does it too: redshift and blueshift

<p>Light is also a wave. For visible light, wavelength shows up as <strong>colour</strong>: red light has the longest wavelength, violet the shortest.</p><ul><li>A light source <strong>moving away</strong> from us has its wavelengths stretched. Everything shifts towards the red end: <strong>redshift</strong>.</li><li>A source <strong>moving towards</strong> us has its wavelengths squashed: <strong>blueshift</strong>.</li></ul><p>A galaxy does not suddenly look red. The shift is small. So how can we measure it? We use the <strong>absorption lines</strong> from our spectroscopy practical. Hydrogen always absorbs at the same wavelengths in a lab (656 nm, 486 nm and 434 nm in visible light). In a galaxy's spectrum we find the <strong>same pattern</strong> of lines, with the same spacing ratios, but slid along towards the red.</p><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/hubble-redshifted-spectrum.jpg" alt="Two absorption spectra, each a rainbow from violet to red with dark lines. In the lower spectrum the same pattern of dark lines is shifted towards the red end" width="600" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: NASA (public domain) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/science/science-behind-the-discoveries/hubble-cosmological-redshift/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p style='font-size:90%;'><strong>Top:</strong> absorption lines from a source that is not moving relative to us. <strong>Bottom:</strong> the same pattern of lines from a galaxy moving away: every line has slid towards the red (longer wavelength). Look at the spacing: the pattern is the same, only its position has changed.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> same pattern of lines + shifted to longer wavelengths = the source is moving <strong>away</strong> from us. The bigger the shift, the faster it is moving away.</p></div>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: reading a shift</summary>
<p>In the lab, a hydrogen line is at 656 nm. In a galaxy's spectrum the same line is at 689 nm. Is the galaxy moving towards us or away? Roughly how big is the shift, as a percentage?</p><hr><p>689 nm is <strong>longer</strong> than 656 nm, so the line has shifted towards red: <strong>redshift</strong>. The galaxy is moving <strong>away</strong> from us.</p><p>Shift = 689 − 656 = 33 nm. 33 ÷ 656 ≈ 0.05, so about <strong>5%</strong>. (For speeds much less than light, 5% of the speed of light is about 15 000 km/s.)</p>
</details>

## Hubble: farther galaxies are moving away faster

<p>This discovery took several people:</p><ul><li><strong>Henrietta Leavitt</strong> (1912) found that a type of pulsing star, a <strong>Cepheid</strong>, has a brightness linked to how fast it pulses. That made Cepheids "standard candles" for measuring <strong>distance</strong>.</li><li><strong>Vesto Slipher</strong> (1912–1917) measured the spectra of fuzzy "spiral nebulae" and found that most were <strong>redshifted</strong>, some by huge amounts.</li><li><strong>Edwin Hubble</strong> used Cepheids to show these nebulae are separate <strong>galaxies</strong>, far outside the Milky Way. In <strong>1929</strong> he plotted their distances against their speeds (from Slipher's and his own redshifts).</li></ul><p>The pattern: almost every galaxy is moving away from us, and the <strong>farther away</strong> a galaxy is, the <strong>faster</strong> it is moving away. Speed is roughly proportional to distance.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u05-hubble-plot.png" alt="Scatter graph of recession speed in km/s against distance in millions of light-years for ten galaxies (illustrative data), with points rising in a straight line through the origin, about 22 km/s for every million light-years" width="600" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p>For this graph, the line rises about <strong>22 km/s for every million light-years</strong> (the real value is close to this).</p><p><strong>Credit where it is due:</strong> Belgian physicist and priest <strong>Georges Lemaître</strong> predicted an expanding universe from Einstein's theory of gravity and linked it to galaxy redshifts in 1927, two years before Hubble's graph.</p>

## Space itself is stretching

<p>Why would far galaxies move away faster? Because <strong>space itself is expanding</strong>. Galaxies are not flying out through space from one spot. The space between them is growing.</p><p><strong>The raisin bread model:</strong> raisins in rising dough. As the dough rises, every raisin moves away from every other raisin. A raisin twice as far away moves away twice as fast, because there is twice as much dough between them stretching. And from <em>any</em> raisin, all the others seem to move away. So seeing every galaxy recede does <strong>not</strong> mean we are at the centre.</p><p>Light travelling through stretching space gets stretched too. That is the best way to picture the redshift of distant galaxies.</p><p><strong>Limits of the model:</strong></p><ul><li>The loaf has an edge and a centre. As far as we know, the universe has neither.</li><li>The loaf grows <em>into</em> the kitchen. Space is not expanding into anything.</li><li>In the loaf, the dough pushes the raisins apart. In the universe nothing pushes the galaxies: the space between them grows. (One way the model <em>does</em> match: the raisins themselves do not grow, just as gravity holds galaxies, stars and you together so they do not stretch.)</li><li>Nearby galaxies can still move towards us. Andromeda's gravity pulls it towards the Milky Way faster than the space between us grows.</li></ul>

## Guided example

<p>Most galaxies show redshift, but the Andromeda Galaxy (2.5 million light-years away) shows <strong>blueshift</strong>. Does this mean the universe is not expanding?</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>No.</strong> Blueshift means Andromeda is moving <strong>towards</strong> us. As we see it from the Sun, it is approaching at about <strong>300 km/s</strong>. Part of that is our own Sun's orbit around the Milky Way carrying us towards Andromeda; for the Milky Way as a whole, the approach is about <strong>110 km/s</strong>.</p><p>At 2.5 million light-years, the Hubble trend predicts a recession speed of only about 2.5 × 22 ≈ 55 km/s. Andromeda and the Milky Way are close enough that their <strong>gravity</strong> pulls them together much faster than the space between them grows.</p><p>The expansion shows up in the pattern for <strong>distant</strong> galaxies, where it wins easily. One nearby exception does not overturn a trend measured across thousands of galaxies, and the exception has a good scientific explanation.</p>
</details>

## Exam skill: Justified explanation

<div class="admonition note"><p class="admonition-title">What a full answer includes</p><ul><li><strong>Claim:</strong> a clear answer to the question in one sentence</li><li><strong>Evidence:</strong> the trend described AND at least two data points quoted from the graph, with units</li><li><strong>Reasoning:</strong> the science that links the evidence to the claim (why does this evidence support this claim?)</li></ul></div>

<p><em>Worked example</em></p>
<p><strong>Worked example (a different context).</strong> A car sounds its horn as it drives past a listener at a steady speed. The graph shows the pitch the listener hears.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u05-car-pitch.png" alt="Graph of pitch heard in Hz against time in s: steady at about 467 Hz until 4 s, drops quickly through 440 Hz at 5 s when the car passes, then steady at about 416 Hz" width="520" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Question:</strong> Explain what the graph shows about the motion of the car.</p><p><strong>Claim:</strong> The car was moving towards the listener for the first 5 seconds and away from the listener after that.</p><p><strong>Evidence:</strong> Before 5 s the pitch heard is steady at about 467 Hz, which is higher than the 440 Hz the horn makes when the car is parked. After 5 s it drops to a steady 416 Hz, lower than 440 Hz. The pitch crosses 440 Hz at 5 s.</p><p><strong>Reasoning:</strong> This is the Doppler effect. When a source moves towards a listener the sound waves are squashed, so the wavelength is shorter and the pitch is higher. When it moves away the waves are stretched and the pitch is lower. So higher-than-440 means approaching and lower-than-440 means receding, and the car passed the listener at 5 s.</p><p><em>Notice: every number has a unit, and the reasoning uses the science (wavelength squashed or stretched), not just "because the graph says so".</em></p>
