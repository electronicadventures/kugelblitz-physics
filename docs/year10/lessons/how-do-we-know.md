## Theory, guess or conspiracy?

<p>In everyday talk, "it's just a theory" means "it's just a guess". In science the word means the opposite.</p><ul><li>A <strong>guess</strong> is an idea with no evidence yet.</li><li>A <strong>scientific theory</strong> is a well-tested explanation. It is supported by many lines of evidence and it makes <strong>predictions</strong> that can be checked.</li><li>A <strong>conspiracy theory</strong> explains away the evidence ("they faked it") and cannot be tested, because any evidence against it is called part of the cover-up.</li></ul><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> you do not have to <em>believe</em> scientists. You can check <em>how</em> they know: what was measured, and what was predicted.</p></div>

## Measuring the Earth with two sticks

<p>Around 240 BCE, <strong>Eratosthenes</strong> heard that at noon on midsummer's day the Sun shone straight down a well in Syene, Egypt. A stick there cast <strong>no shadow</strong>. On the same day in Alexandria, about 800 km north, a stick <strong>did</strong> cast a shadow: the Sun was 7.2° away from straight overhead.</p><p>Sunlight arrives in parallel rays. The only way the two shadows can differ is if the ground is <strong>curved</strong>. The 7.2° is also the angle between the two cities measured at Earth's centre.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u01-eratosthenes.png" alt="Diagram of Eratosthenes&#x27; method: parallel sunlight, a stick with no shadow at Syene, a stick with a shadow at Alexandria, and a 7.2 degree angle at Earth&#x27;s centre" width="560" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: how big is the Earth?</summary>
<p>7.2° is what fraction of a full circle? Use it to estimate Earth's circumference.</p><hr><p>A full circle is 360°. 360 ÷ 7.2 = <strong>50</strong>, so the distance between the cities is 1/50 of the way around Earth.</p><p>Circumference ≈ 50 × 800 km = <strong>40 000 km</strong>.</p><p><strong>Check:</strong> today's measured value is about 40 000 km around the poles. Two sticks, a shadow and some geometry, more than 2000 years ago.</p>
</details>

## The transit of Venus: the 1700s space race

<p><strong>Kepler's laws</strong> (1609–1619) showed that planets move in ellipses, move faster when closer to the Sun, and that the farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year (T<sup>2</sup> ∝ r<sup>3</sup>). This gave the <em>relative</em> spacing of the planets: Jupiter is about 5.2 times as far from the Sun as Earth, so its year is about 11.9 Earth years. But nobody knew the <strong>actual distance in kilometres</strong>. Without one real distance, the whole map of the solar system had no scale.</p><p>A <strong>transit of Venus</strong> is when Venus passes directly between Earth and the Sun, and looks like a small black dot crossing the Sun. Transits come in pairs 8 years apart, then not again for over 100 years.</p><ul><li><strong>1639:</strong> Jeremiah Horrocks and William Crabtree in England were the first to observe one.</li><li><strong>1716:</strong> Edmond Halley showed how to use a transit to find the Earth–Sun distance: observers <strong>far apart on Earth</strong> see Venus cross the Sun on slightly different paths and at slightly different times. That difference, plus geometry, gives the distance. This shift with viewing position is called <strong>parallax</strong>.</li><li><strong>1761 and 1769:</strong> Britain, France and other nations sent observers all over the world.</li></ul><p><img src="../../img/y10u01-venus-transit.png" alt="Diagram: two observers far apart on Earth see Venus against different points on the Sun" width="600" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Guillaume Le Gentil</strong>, a French astronomer, sailed for India in 1760 to see the 1761 transit. War delayed him and he was still at sea on the day, on a rolling ship where accurate timing was impossible. He decided to wait 8 years for the next one, and built an observatory at Pondicherry. The skies were clear for weeks beforehand, then clouds covered the Sun for the transit and cleared soon after. He sailed home and reached Paris in 1771, after more than 11 years away, to find he had been <strong>declared dead</strong>: his relatives had divided up his property and his place at the Academy of Sciences had been filled. He had to go to court to get his life back.</p><p>In 1769 Britain sent Lieutenant <strong>James Cook</strong> on HMS <em>Endeavour</em> to Tahiti with astronomer <strong>Charles Green</strong>. They observed the whole transit. (The voyage also carried secret orders to search for a southern continent, which led to Cook charting the east coast of Australia in 1770.) Combining results from around the world gave an Earth–Sun distance within a few per cent of today's value of about <strong>150 million km</strong>.</p>

## Light-years: looking out is looking back

<p>Light is fast (300 000 km every second) but not instant. A <strong>light-year</strong> is a <strong>distance</strong>: how far light travels in one year, about 9.46 × 10<sup>12</sup> km (9.46 trillion km).</p><p>Because light takes time to reach us, we always see things as they <em>were</em> when the light left them. You see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes 20 seconds ago. On about 18 November 2026, the Voyager 1 spacecraft (launched 1977) becomes the first human-made object to be <strong>one light-day</strong> away: a radio message takes a full day to reach it.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u01-light-travel.png" alt="Bar chart, log scale, of how long light takes to reach us from the Moon (1.3 s), Sun (8 min 20 s), Neptune (about 4 hours), Voyager 1 (1 day), Proxima Centauri (4.2 years), the centre of the Milky Way (about 26 000 years) and the Andromeda Galaxy (2.5 million years)" width="620" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Distances to stars</strong> are measured with the same parallax idea, using Earth's orbit as the baseline. A nearby star seems to shift a tiny amount against very distant stars between January and July. Friedrich Bessel made the first accepted measurement in 1838.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u01-parallax.png" alt="Stellar parallax diagram: Earth in January and July on opposite sides of the Sun, lines through a nearby star point to different background stars" width="480" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><p><strong>Photos of the whole Milky Way?</strong> Nobody has one. We are inside it. Every "photo" of the whole galaxy from outside is an artist's illustration based on measurements of where its stars and gas are, like mapping a forest from the inside.</p>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: how far is a light-year?</summary>
<p>Light travels 300 000 km each second. There are about 3.16 × 10<sup>7</sup> seconds in a year. How far is one light-year?</p><hr><p>distance = speed × time = 300 000 km/s × 3.16 × 10<sup>7</sup> s = 9.48 × 10<sup>12</sup> km.</p><p>That rounds to the accepted value of about <strong>9.46 × 10<sup>12</sup> km</strong> (the small difference is rounding of the speed of light).</p>
</details>

## Guided example

<p>Le Gentil was ready in Pondicherry in 1769 but clouds hid the Sun. Why could one observer in one place <strong>never</strong> have measured the Earth–Sun distance by himself, even with clear skies?</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p>The method needs <strong>parallax</strong>: comparing what <em>two or more</em> observers, far apart on Earth, see at the same time. One observer gives one view of Venus against the Sun. You need a second view from a known distance away to compare paths and timings, then geometry gives the distance. That is why nations sent observers to places as far apart as Tahiti, India, Siberia and Canada, and why accurate clocks and knowing each observer's exact position mattered so much.</p>
</details>
