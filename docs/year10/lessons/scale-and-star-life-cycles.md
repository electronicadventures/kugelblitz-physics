# The scale of the universe and the life of a star

<span class="checklist-codes">AC9S10U03</span> <span class="checklist-codes">AC9S10I05</span>

## Hook

<p><strong>Hold up a grain of sand.</strong> If the Sun were shrunk to the size of that grain (about 1 mm across):</p><ul><li>Earth would be a speck of dust about 10 cm away.</li><li>The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, would be another grain of sand about <strong>30 km</strong> away.</li><li>The Milky Way, our galaxy, would be about <strong>700 000 km</strong> across, nearly twice the distance to the Moon.</li></ul><p>And every atom of calcium in your bones and iron in your blood was made inside a star that died before the Sun was born.</p><p><strong>Think–pair–share (2 min):</strong> which of those facts is hardest to believe? What would you want to measure to check it?</p>

## The scale ladder

<p>Sizes in space are so different that ordinary numbers stop working. We use <strong>scientific notation</strong>: a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten. Each step up in the power means <strong>10 times bigger</strong>.</p><p><img src="../../img/y10u03-scale-ladder.png" alt="Bar chart on a log scale of diameters: asteroid Bennu about 0.5 km, Earth 1.3 × 10^4 km, Jupiter 1.4 × 10^5 km, Sun 1.4 × 10^6 km, Betelgeuse about 10^9 km, Milky Way about 100 000 light-years, Local Group about 10 million light-years, observable universe about 93 billion light-years" width="640" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"></p><ul><li>A <strong>planet</strong> orbits a star; a <strong>star</strong> is a huge ball of hot gas that makes its own light.</li><li>A <strong>galaxy</strong> is a collection of billions of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.</li><li>Galaxies gather in <strong>groups and clusters</strong>. The Milky Way belongs to the <strong>Local Group</strong>, with the Andromeda Galaxy and dozens of smaller galaxies.</li><li>The <strong>observable universe</strong> is everything whose light has had time to reach us.</li></ul><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> when the scale has a log axis, each tick is a jump of many times, not a step of the same size. On the chart above the Sun looks close to Jupiter, but it is 10 times wider.</p></div>

<details class="example"><summary>Worked example: how many Earths across the Sun?</summary>
<p>Earth is 1.3 × 10<sup>4</sup> km across. The Sun is 1.4 × 10<sup>6</sup> km across. About how many Earths would fit across the Sun?</p><hr><p>Divide the numbers and subtract the powers: (1.4 ÷ 1.3) × 10<sup>6 − 4</sup> ≈ 1.1 × 10<sup>2</sup> ≈ <strong>110 Earths</strong>.</p><p><strong>Now try:</strong> Betelgeuse is about 1 × 10<sup>9</sup> km across. How many Suns across is that? (1 × 10<sup>9</sup> ÷ 1.4 × 10<sup>6</sup> ≈ 700 Suns.)</p>
</details>

## Birth and the main sequence

<p>Every star starts in a <strong>nebula</strong>: a huge, cold cloud of gas (mostly hydrogen and helium) and dust.</p><ol><li><strong>Gravity</strong> pulls part of the cloud together. As it squeezes in, it heats up. This hot, shrinking ball is a <strong>protostar</strong>.</li><li>When the centre reaches about 10 million K (kelvin; at these temperatures K and °C are almost the same number), hydrogen nuclei start to join together to make helium. This is <strong>nuclear fusion</strong>, and it releases huge amounts of energy. A star is born.</li><li>The star is now a <strong>main-sequence star</strong>. Gravity pulls inwards; the pressure from the hot gas and energy from fusion pushes outwards. The two are in <strong>balance</strong>, so the star stays the same size for a long time.</li></ol><p>Stars spend about 90% of their lives on the main sequence. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years into a main-sequence life of about 10 billion years.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Surprise:</strong> bigger stars have <em>shorter</em> lives. A star with more mass has a hotter, more squeezed core, so it burns through its hydrogen much faster. A star 20 times the Sun's mass lasts only about 10 million years.</p></div>

## Two endings: mass decides

<p>When the hydrogen in the core runs low, fusion slows, gravity wins for a while, and the core shrinks and heats up. The outer layers swell and cool. What happens next depends on the star's <strong>mass</strong>.</p><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/missions/webb/outreach/migrated/2018/STScI-01HVSD3PAQ79QDG9FMDZKVFTQ2.png/jcr:content/renditions/cq5dam.web.1280.1280.png" alt="NASA infographic of the two paths of a star&#x27;s life. Sun-like stars: star-forming nebula, protostars, Sun-like star, red giant, planetary nebula, white dwarf. Massive stars: star-forming nebula, protostars, massive star, red supergiant, supernova, then a neutron star or black hole. Both paths return material to a star-forming nebula" width="660" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: NASA, Night Sky Network (public domain) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/life-cycles-of-sun-like-and-massive-stars/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p><strong>Reading the diagram:</strong> the "Sun-like star" and the "massive star" are both <strong>main-sequence stars</strong>. The diagram does not give a number, but the dividing line is about <strong>8 times the Sun's mass</strong>. Notice the arrows back to the star-forming nebula: a dying star's gas is recycled into new stars (section 4).</p><p><strong>Low and average-mass stars (like the Sun):</strong> the star swells into a <strong>red giant</strong>. Its outer layers drift away into space as a glowing shell called a <strong>planetary nebula</strong> (nothing to do with planets; it just looked like one in old telescopes). The core left behind is a <strong>white dwarf</strong>: about the size of Earth, very hot and dim, slowly cooling for billions of years.</p><p><strong>High-mass stars:</strong> the star swells into a <strong>red supergiant</strong> (like Betelgeuse). Its core fuses heavier and heavier elements until it makes iron. Fusing iron does not release energy, so the core suddenly collapses and the star explodes as a <strong>supernova</strong>, briefly outshining a whole galaxy. What is left is either a <strong>neutron star</strong> (a city-sized ball of neutrons) or, if the leftover core is heavy enough, a <strong>black hole</strong>, where gravity is so strong that not even light can escape.</p>

## We are made of star stuff

<p>Right after the big bang, the universe had almost only <strong>hydrogen and helium</strong>. So where did everything else come from?</p><ul><li>Fusion in stars made the elements up to <strong>iron</strong>: carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, calcium and more.</li><li>Elements <strong>heavier than iron</strong> need even more energy. <strong>Most of the gold</strong> and other very heavy elements (such as platinum and uranium) were made in <strong>supernovae</strong> and in collisions of <strong>neutron stars</strong>. (Some heavy elements are also built up slowly inside giant stars.)</li><li>These explosions throw the new elements into space. They mix into new nebulae, which form new stars, planets and, eventually, people.</li></ul><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Key idea:</strong> the carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe and the gold in a ring were all made in stars that lived and died before the Sun formed. You are, literally, made of star stuff.</p></div>

## Card sort: build the life cycle

<p>In pairs, you get 11 cards: <em>nebula, protostar, main-sequence star, red giant, red supergiant, planetary nebula, supernova, white dwarf, neutron star, black hole</em>, and a blank card.</p><ol><li><strong>Sort</strong> the cards into a flow chart with one start and two endings. Put arrows between them.</li><li>On the blank card, write <strong>what decides the path</strong>, and place it where the paths split.</li><li>Under each card, add one short fact (e.g. "fusion of hydrogen into helium", "explosion spreads heavy elements").</li><li><strong>Challenge:</strong> put these in order of size, smallest first: <em>neutron star, white dwarf, Sun, red giant, red supergiant</em>.</li></ol><p>Check your flow chart against the NASA diagram in section 3 (it calls main-sequence stars "Sun-like star" and "massive star"), then photograph it or copy it into your book.</p><p><em>Challenge answer: neutron star (about 20 km) &lt; white dwarf (about Earth-sized) &lt; Sun &lt; red giant &lt; red supergiant.</em></p>

## Guided example

<p>Two stars form in the same nebula at the same time. Star X has 0.9 times the Sun's mass. Star Y has 15 times the Sun's mass. Which star will die first, and how will each one end?</p>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answer</summary>
<p><strong>Step 1: what decides the path?</strong> Mass. The dividing line is about 8 times the Sun's mass.</p><p><strong>Step 2: star X (0.9 × Sun).</strong> A little less mass than the Sun. It fuses hydrogen a little more slowly, so it lives a bit longer than the Sun's 10 billion years (roughly 13–15 billion years). Eventually: red giant → planetary nebula → white dwarf.</p><p><strong>Step 3: star Y (15 × Sun).</strong> High mass. Its core is hotter and more squeezed, so it uses its fuel fast and lives only a few tens of millions of years. Then: red supergiant → supernova → most likely a neutron star (a black hole if the leftover core is heavy enough).</p><p><strong>Answer:</strong> star Y dies first, even though it has much more fuel, because it burns it far faster.</p>
</details>

## Exit questions

<ol><li>Explain what keeps a main-sequence star from collapsing or blowing apart.</li><li>Why does a star with more mass have a shorter life?</li><li>In one or two sentences, explain what "we are made of star stuff" means.</li></ol>
<details class="success"><summary>Show answers</summary>
<ol><li>Gravity pulls the star's gas inwards. Pressure from the hot gas, powered by energy from fusion in the core, pushes outwards. The two forces balance.</li><li>More mass squeezes the core harder, making it hotter, so fusion happens much faster. The star uses up its hydrogen sooner, even though it started with more.</li><li>Most elements in our bodies (like carbon, oxygen, calcium and iron) were made by fusion in stars, and most of the heaviest ones in supernovae and neutron-star collisions. Dying stars spread them into space, where they became part of the Sun, Earth and us.</li></ol>
</details>
