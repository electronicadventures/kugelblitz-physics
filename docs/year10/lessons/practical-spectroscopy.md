# Practical: can light tell us what stars are made of?

!!! abstract "By the end of this lesson you should be able to"
    - Use a spectroscope safely to observe continuous and line spectra.

## Hook

<p><strong>Back to the conspiracy board.</strong> Last lesson we busted the size of Earth, the distance to the Sun and the distance to the stars. One claim survived:</p><p style='font-size:115%;'><em>"We know what stars are made of."</em> <em>(Nobody has ever brought a sample home.)</em></p><p>In 1835 the French philosopher Auguste Comte said we would <strong>never</strong> know what stars are made of. Less than 30 years later, scientists had worked it out, without leaving Earth.</p><p>Here is the strangest part of the story. In <strong>1868</strong>, astronomers studying light from the Sun during a solar eclipse saw a bright <strong>yellow line</strong> that matched no element known on Earth. They named the new element <strong>helium</strong>, after <em>helios</em>, Greek for Sun. Helium was not found on Earth until <strong>1895</strong>, 27 years later.</p><p><strong>Today's question:</strong> how can a line of coloured light tell you what something is made of? You will split light yourself and find out.</p>

## The practical

<p>To observe the spectra of different light sources with a handheld spectroscope, and to decide whether each one is a <strong>continuous spectrum</strong> (a smooth rainbow), a <strong>line spectrum</strong> (separate bright lines) or a mix of both.</p><p>A <strong>spectroscope</strong> is a tool that splits light into its colours. Inside it is a <strong>diffraction grating</strong>: a clear sheet with thousands of tiny, evenly spaced lines that spread light out like a prism does.</p>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Do all light sources give the same spectrum? Can the spectrum be used to tell which gas is glowing inside a tube?</p>

<p><strong>You will need:</strong></p><ul><li>handheld spectroscopes or diffraction grating glasses/slides (one per pair)</li><li>incandescent (filament) light bulb in a lamp</li><li>white LED torch or lamp</li><li>fluorescent tube or compact fluorescent lamp</li><li>gas discharge (spectrum) tubes, e.g. hydrogen, helium, neon, and the spectrum tube power supply (teacher-operated only)</li><li>sheet of white paper (for reflected sunlight or daylight, optional)</li><li>coloured pencils</li><li>printed or projected copy of the reference spectra (Making sense tab)</li></ul>

<div class="admonition warning"><p class="admonition-title">Safety</p><ul><li><strong>Spectrum tube power supply uses very high voltage (thousands of volts):</strong> Teacher-operated only. Students never touch the power supply, leads or tube holder. The power supply is switched off before any tube is changed.</li><li><strong>Discharge tubes and lamps get hot; glass tubes can break:</strong> Do not touch tubes or bulbs. The teacher lets tubes cool before changing them, and uses tubes in an enclosed holder. Report any broken glass; do not pick it up.</li><li><strong>Looking at the Sun, even through a spectroscope, can permanently damage your eyes:</strong> NEVER point a spectroscope at the Sun. For sunlight, point it at white paper lit by the Sun or at bright sky well away from the Sun.</li><li><strong>Some lamps (fluorescent, mercury and some discharge tubes) give off a little ultraviolet (UV) light:</strong> Glass blocks most UV, but view each source for short periods only (under a minute) and keep about 30 cm or more away.</li><li><strong>Darkened room: trips and bumps:</strong> Bags under benches, stools tucked in, stay at your station while the lights are dimmed.</li></ul></div>

### Method

<ol><li><strong>Practise first.</strong> Hold the spectroscope with the slit end towards a light and look through the other end. Turn it until the spectrum appears to the side of the slit, spread out from violet to red.</li><li><strong>Incandescent bulb.</strong> Look at the bulb (not too close). In the results table, sketch and colour what you see and decide: continuous, lines or both?</li><li><strong>White LED.</strong> Repeat. Look carefully: are all the colours equally bright, or is there a bright blue part?</li><li><strong>Fluorescent tube.</strong> Repeat. Look for a few bright lines sitting on top of a fainter rainbow.</li><li><strong>Discharge tubes (teacher-operated).</strong> The teacher switches on one tube at a time. Look from your seat or move to the marked viewing spot. For each gas, sketch the bright lines in the right positions and colours.</li><li><strong>Optional: sunlight.</strong> Point the spectroscope at white paper in sunlight (never at the Sun). With a good spectroscope you may see a few thin dark lines across the rainbow.</li><li>Return all spectroscopes, then go to the Making sense tab.</li></ol>

### Making sense of it

<p><strong>Why do glowing gases give lines?</strong></p><p>Electrons in an atom can only have certain amounts of energy, called <strong>energy levels</strong>. Think of a ladder: you can stand on a rung, but not between rungs.</p><ul><li>When an atom <strong>absorbs energy</strong> (from heat or electricity), an electron jumps <strong>up</strong> to a higher level.</li><li>When the electron drops back <strong>down</strong>, the atom gives out that energy as light. The size of the drop decides the colour: a big drop gives violet or blue light, a small drop gives red light.</li><li>Each element has its own set of levels, so it gives out its own set of colours. The pattern of lines is the element's <strong>fingerprint</strong>.</li></ul><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/missions/webb/outreach/migrated/2021/STScI-01F8QNQHJMTWF4GXSD2RHARQEZ.png" alt="Infographic: electrons in a hydrogen atom dropping between energy levels give out light at 410, 434, 486 and 656 nanometres, shown as four bright lines in hydrogen&#x27;s emission spectrum" width="600" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, Leah Hustak (STScI) (public domain) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/spectroscopy-101-how-absorption-and-emission-spectra-work/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p>Hydrogen's four visible lines are at <strong>410, 434, 486 and 656 nm</strong>. The biggest of these four drops gives violet light (410 nm); the smallest gives red light (656 nm).</p><p><strong>Three kinds of spectrum</strong></p><ul><li><strong>Continuous spectrum:</strong> a smooth rainbow with no gaps. Made by a hot, dense object such as a bulb filament or the inside of a star.</li><li><strong>Emission spectrum:</strong> bright coloured lines on a dark background. Made by a hot, thin gas (like your discharge tubes).</li><li><strong>Absorption spectrum:</strong> a rainbow with thin dark lines missing. Made when white light passes through a cooler gas. The gas absorbs exactly the colours it would emit, so the dark lines are in the <em>same places</em> as that gas's bright lines.</li></ul><figure style="margin:1em 0;"><img src="https://assets.science.nasa.gov/content/dam/science/missions/webb/outreach/migrated/2021/STScI-01FFJCF85TXGNKZ8GSPEGAH4JB.png" alt="Infographic: a star&#x27;s light shines through a cloud of gas. Below it, the star&#x27;s continuous spectrum, the gas cloud&#x27;s emission spectrum (bright lines) and the absorption spectrum (dark lines in the rainbow) are shown as pictures and graphs" width="620" style="max-width:100%;" loading="lazy"><figcaption style="font-size:0.7rem;">Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, Leah Hustak (STScI) (public domain) · <a href="https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/types-of-spectra-continuous-emission-and-absorption/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">source</a></figcaption></figure><p>Look at where you stand in the diagram: looking at the star <em>through</em> the cloud gives the absorption spectrum; looking at the glowing cloud from the side gives the emission spectrum. The bright lines and the dark lines are at the same colours.</p><p>A star's hot inner layers make a continuous spectrum. That light passes through the star's cooler outer layers, which absorb some colours. So <strong>starlight is an absorption spectrum</strong>, and the dark lines tell us which elements are in the star.</p><div class="admonition tip"><p style='margin:0;'><strong>Conspiracy busted:</strong> nobody needs a sample. The light brings the evidence to us. Every element has a fixed line pattern that we can measure in a lab on Earth and then find in starlight. That is how we know the Sun is mostly hydrogen and helium.</p></div><p><strong>Questions</strong> (answer in your book, then check)</p><ol><li>Which sources gave a continuous spectrum? Which gave lines? What do the line sources have in common?<details style='margin:4px 0 10px 0;padding:6px 10px;background:#f9fafb;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;border-radius:6px;'><summary style='cursor:pointer;color:#1f5fa8;'><strong>Show model answer</strong></summary><p style='margin:6px 0 0 0;'>The incandescent bulb (hot solid filament) gave a continuous spectrum. Why?<details style='margin:4px 0 10px 0;padding:6px 10px;background:#f9fafb;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;border-radius:6px;'><summary style='cursor:pointer;color:#1f5fa8;'><strong>Show model answer</strong></summary><p style='margin:6px 0 0 0;'>Each element has a different set of energy levels, so its electrons make different-sized drops and give out different colours. Different line patterns mean different elements.</p></details></li><li>The fluorescent tube showed bright lines on top of a faint rainbow. Suggest why. (Hint: the tube contains a little mercury gas and is coated inside with a white powder.)<details style='margin:4px 0 10px 0;padding:6px 10px;background:#f9fafb;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;border-radius:6px;'><summary style='cursor:pointer;color:#1f5fa8;'><strong>Show model answer</strong></summary><p style='margin:6px 0 0 0;'>The mercury gas gives bright lines (including violet, green and yellow).
